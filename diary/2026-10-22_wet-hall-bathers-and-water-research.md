## Problem Statement

The owner asked whether switching off the hypochlorite drives every pool's pH down, and whether switching off the mains water drains the pools. The first was only a rule with a narrow test; the second showed the model was wrong: the pool pumps depended on mains water, so cutting the mains stopped them at once and nothing ran down. The owner then asked for the wet hall to be modelled properly in the world, its machines redesigned to fit the model, and its pipes reconnected. Before the water's chemistry and biology are made richer, the owner also wanted research on existing models (chemistry after failures, and how the water looks when it goes wrong) and a count of the pools' unseen users, opening 08:00 to 21:00 with real peak times.

## Our Approach

Model first, then the machines. The wet hall became a block in the world with its own state (break tank, booster set, meter, backwash tank), stepped with the pools so the make-up the pools draw comes out of it and their backwash goes into it. The pools' pumps now need only power. The booster set's low-level trip latches until someone resets it, which gives the owner the indicator they asked for: a pump failed for want of water. Two research agents ran in the background while this was built. The bathers are a separate, seeded, saved model that nothing downstream uses yet.

## Outcomes

- The mains-off run behaves as a real plant would, in three stages: the break tank carries the pools for some hours, the booster set trips on low water, then each pool's balance tank runs down over days until its pumps trip. Nothing empties: only evaporation and backwash take water out.
- The research found no published model of a pool going wrong, but good parts: the US EPA's MIT-licensed chloramine simulator (Jafvert and Valentine), per-bather inputs (about 80 mg nitrogen, 3 to 11 g chlorine demand, 30 litres of dilution), Chick–Watson kill rates, and ocean-colour formulas for the water's look. It also found the current pH, redox, chlorine decay and chloramine rules to be crude, and listed how.
- A simulated week of bathers gives 260 to 500 lounge visits a day and 1,800 to 2,600 Olympic, near the research's figures; the jacuzzi is often full in the evenings.
- Lesson: an experiment run before a test is written finds model errors that a test written to the model would have enshrined (the pumps' dependence on mains water).

## Afterwards: bathers follow the water

The owner pointed out that people will not come to a pool whose water is poor, too cold or hot, acid or alkaline, or over-chlorinated. Each pool now has an appeal, 0 to 1 (`poolAppeal` in `bathers.ts`, from PWTAG's ranges and HSG282's spa limit): closed (0) if its water is not circulating, its hall is dark with no daylight, its free chlorine is under 0.5 mg/l, or a spa is over 40 °C; otherwise the product of its temperature (as wanted within 1.5 °C of target, nobody 6 °C off), pH (7.0–7.6, nobody at 6.5 or 8.2), free chlorine (1–3, nobody at 5) and combined chlorine (to 0.5, nobody at 1.5). Arrivals scale with it, bathers leave sooner from a poor pool, and a closed pool is cleared within a minute or two. In ordinary running every pool keeps an appeal of 1; with the hypochlorite gone, the lounge pool closes within hours and is emptied. The water does not yet answer the bathers (their chlorine demand and nitrogen), so the loop is one way for now.

The owner then made lighting a hard requirement: with its lights out nobody uses a pool, however good the water and whatever the daylight, so the skylit halls no longer count as usable by daylight alone. And the hall's air matters as well as the water's: each pool hall now has an air temperature (`hallAir`, `WORLD_VERSION` 10), held at its design figure (a pool hall's about 1 °C over its water, 31 °C for the lounge and 29 °C for the Olympic; 30 °C for the jacuzzi room and 28 °C for the footbath passage) while its plant room has air handling and heating, and cooling toward an unheated 18 °C over hours without them. A hall 2 °C off is as wanted; 8 °C colder or 6 °C hotter, nobody comes. Smells (musty, stagnant and worse) will join the appeal once the water has biology.

## Afterwards: heat

The owner asked for the world to have an outside temperature, 12 °C for now but able to rise and fall (a cold snap that needs auxiliary boilers, or pools closed to keep the Olympic warm), and for each pool and its hall to have real thermal bulk; corridors left out. `src/game/plant/thermal.ts` is a lumped two-body model per hall: the water (its volume's heat capacity) and the hall's air with its fabric (ten times the air's own, a tuned figure). The water is heated by a thermostat up to its heat exchanger's output (2.5 times its design load, to warm up again) while it circulates and the heating runs, and loses heat by evaporation (in proportion to the vapour-pressure difference, calibrated to the sizing's evaporation at design, with the hall's humidity 60 % while its air handling runs and 90 % when still, so an unventilated pool does not chill itself far below the outside), by convection to the air, to the ground and to cold make-up. The air is heated by its air handling's coils by thermostat, and by the water, and loses heat through the envelope (U 0.35) and by infiltration (0.5 air changes an hour). The world has `ambient` and a `weather/ambient` action (`WORLD_VERSION` 11); each pool reports its heating in kW, the boilers' load to come. In ordinary running the Olympic takes about 280 kW and the lounge 50; with the heating off the jacuzzi falls about 12 °C in its first day, the lounge 5 and the Olympic 2, and everything settles within a degree or two of the outside; recovery takes from 12 hours (the footbath) to a week (the Olympic); a −5 °C cold snap is held, at nearly twice the heat.
