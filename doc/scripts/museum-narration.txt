THE MUSEUM: NARRATION

HALL 0: WELCOME, ARRIVING BY THE LIFT

1. Welcome to Plantrooms. Imagine you've just jumped into a swimming pool: It's simplest thing in the world. You dive in, and the water is simply there, clean, clear and warm. You never had to think about it. That is the point.

2. No, this isn't a real pool, but behind every pool, underneath the tiles lies a world of pumps, filters, boilers and careful chemistry, running all day and all night so that your swim is safe and pleasant. Please join us in the world behind the poolrooms, follow the arrows and discover the plantrooms.

HALL 0: WELCOME, ARRIVING FROM HALL 9

3. Welcome back. You have seen what lives behind the tiles. The next time you dive in you won't take any of this for granted. Thank you for visiting Plantrooms. Please, sign the guestbook and let us know what you think.

HALL 1: WARM WATER

4. A swimming pool is a huge bath that is always going cold. Every hour it loses heat to the air, to the ground and to the swimmers themselves. Keeping it warm is a chain: gas burns in a boiler, hot water carries the heat away, and an exchanger hands it to the pool without the two waters ever mixing. Everything in this hall works. Turn the heating on, and watch how slowly a whole pool warms.

HALL 2: CLEAN WATER

5. Every swimmer brings something with them: dirt, sweat, skin, sun cream, and worse. The plant takes it all out again, all day and all night. It strains the water through a bed of sand, and when the sand is full it washes it out by running the water backwards. Then ozone and ultraviolet light finish the job. Watch the gauges, and you can see the filter filling up.

HALL 3: SAFE WATER

6. Clear water is not the same as safe water. Germs are too small to see, so the plant keeps a little chlorine in the pool all the time, measures it every second, and adds more as the swimmers use it up. Too little, and the water is unsafe. Too much, and it stings. Send in a crowd of swimmers, and watch the controller keep up.

HALL 4: THE CHEMISTRY OF POOLS

7. Keeping a pool safe is a balancing act, and every chemical you add changes more than one thing. In this hall, you are the pool's chemist. Test the water, work out what it needs, and dose it. But go carefully: this pool holds five hundred tonnes of water, and it takes a while to answer.

HALL 5: A DAY IN THE LIFE

8. Before the first swimmer arrives, somebody has already been round the pool. For a hundred years the day has run the same way: open up, empty the skimmers, test the water, take the deliveries, backwash the filters, lock up. The tools have changed, from a chalkboard to a pager. The skimmer basket has not. Walk the photographs from the nineteen-twenties to today, and then empty the skimmer yourself.

HALL 6: THE WORLD OF PUMPS

9. Nothing in a swimming pool moves by itself. Every drop of water you have seen today was pushed there by a pump. A pool needs pumps of every size, from ones that measure out chemicals a drop at a time to ones that could empty the pool in an afternoon. Press a pump's button, and watch the tank.

HALL 7: GROUT, THE UNSUNG HERO OF THE AQUATICS WORLD

10. Welcome. Please, lower your voice. You are standing in the presence of grout. Every tile in every swimming pool you have ever loved has been held in place, quietly, faithfully, by this amazing substance. Take your time certainly Grout has.

11. Lime and sand. From about eighteen-forty, the Victorians set their glazed tiles close, with the finest of joints, and lime filled them, setting slowly as it drew carbon dioxide from the very air. It was there first.

12. In eighteen twenty-four, Joseph Aspdin patented Portland cement. By the eighteen-forties it was burned hotter, and became the cement we would know. For a century it held the public baths of Britain together. It set the standard. And then, it set.

13. Where the joints grew wider, three millimetres and more, cement took sand into its keeping, and went where finer grouts could not. It filled the gaps others could not.

14. For the finest joints, the glazed tile and the mosaic, about two millimetres on a wall, cement was used alone, unsanded, unadorned. It asked for nothing.

15. The nineteen-seventies. Britain chose its bathroom suites in colours, and grout was pigmented to match them. Avocado was among the decade's defining shades. The museum presents this specimen without comment.

16. From about the nineteen-sixties, installers mixed their grout with a latex admixture in place of water. In time it came ready mixed from the factory, and today it is the most popular grout of all. It made cement better, and did not pretend otherwise.

17. Furan resin. From the nineteen-forties, a grout of great chemical resistance, trusted in dairies, in chemical plants, and in some pools. Furan grouts are generally black. The museum regrets that its specimen is not.

18. Epoxy. Its emulsions came in the nineteen-forties; true epoxy grouts were in general use by the sixties and seventies. Class R G, under B S E N thirteen eight eight eight: the pool's choice where the water is soft, or aggressive. Some are rated even for the sea. It is what the water deserves.

19. Today. Class C G two W A: improved cement, absorbing less water, resisting the scrub of brush and foot, often guarding itself against mould. The old way, made as good as it can be.

20. And finally, a confession. This is not grout. It is a movement joint: a flexible sealant where the pool must be allowed to move, at its edges and over its structure's own joints. Not grout. But grout could not do its work without it.

21. Thank you for visiting. As you go back out into the world, and into the water, spare a thought for the joints between the tiles. They never ask to be noticed. That is why we notice them.

HALL 8: NEVER AGAIN, PUTTING SAFETY FIRST

22. Two of the chemicals that keep a pool safe must never meet. Mixed, they make chlorine gas. This is the story of a morning when they did. Each photograph tells part of it: press the button at its lectern to hear what happened.

23. Pellingford Baths, a spring morning in 1997. At seventeen minutes to eight a chemical tanker pulls into the loading bay. It is early. The duty technician has not yet started his shift, and the driver has never been here before. Nobody comes out to meet him.

24. Two fill points stand side by side in an open recess: one for acid, one for sodium hypochlorite. They have the same coupling, and their labels faded long ago. The driver connects his acid hose to the hypochlorite fill. Four hundred litres of acid go into the bulk hypochlorite tank.

25. Mixed, acid and hypochlorite give off chlorine: a choking, yellow-green gas, heavier than air. It rises from the tank's vent into the plant room, and the air handling draws it towards the pool hall. The plant room's gas detector should have raised the alarm. It had been disconnected, weeks before, after false alarms.

26. A lifeguard clears the pool. Swimmers and staff stand in the car park in foil blankets while fire crews in gas-tight suits go in. A handful are treated for breathing difficulties. Nobody dies. But there was no plan, and nobody had practised one.

27. Weeks later, the inspectors report. There was no risk assessment for deliveries. The staff had not been trained to handle the chemicals. Nobody had checked the contractor. The council is fined forty thousand pounds, and the baths are shut for six weeks.

28. Pellingford Baths reopened with its fill points rebuilt: a different coupling for each chemical, each in its own locked cabinet, clearly labelled. Every delivery is met. The gas detector works, and the stores are apart. The fix is to make the mistake impossible. Never again.

HALL 9: THE WHOLE PLANT

29. This is the whole plant: every part you have met today, working together. Heat, filters, chlorine, chemistry and pumps, all at once. And when something goes wrong, the plant tells you, if you know how to listen. Read the alarms, follow the mimic, and find the fault.
