THE MUSEUM: NARRATION

HALL 5: A DAY IN THE LIFE, WHAT'S IN THE SKIMMER?

1. Right. Let's see what the pool's been hiding today.

2. A clump of hair. Hair floats, so the skimmer catches lots of it. Yuck.

3. Leaves, blown in through the doors and left to stew. They smell of pond.

4. A dead rat. It came up through the drains, and the drains won. Hold it by the tail.

5. A dildo. Waterproof, as advertised. Nobody has asked at lost property.

6. A butt plug. How it got into the skimmer is a question for the CCTV, and nobody wants to ask the CCTV.

7. More hair. There is always more hair.

8. Skimmer cleared! You took out six things. Lovely.

HALL 7: GROUT, THE UNSUNG HERO OF THE AQUATICS WORLD

9. Welcome. Please, lower your voice. You are standing in the presence of grout. Every tile in every swimming pool you have ever loved has been held in place, quietly, faithfully, by the substance you are about to meet. Take your time. Grout has.

10. Lime and sand. From about eighteen-forty, the Victorians set their glazed tiles close, with the finest of joints, and lime filled them, setting slowly as it drew carbon dioxide from the very air. It was there first.

11. In eighteen twenty-four, Joseph Aspdin patented Portland cement. By the eighteen-forties it was burned hotter, and became the cement we would know. For a century it held the public baths of Britain together. It set the standard. And then, it set.

12. Where the joints grew wider, three millimetres and more, cement took sand into its keeping, and went where finer grouts could not. It filled the gaps others could not.

13. For the finest joints, the glazed tile and the mosaic, about two millimetres on a wall, cement was used alone, unsanded, unadorned. It asked for nothing.

14. The nineteen-seventies. Britain chose its bathroom suites in colours, and grout was pigmented to match them. Avocado was among the decade's defining shades. The museum presents this specimen without comment.

15. From about the nineteen-sixties, installers mixed their grout with a latex admixture in place of water. In time it came ready mixed from the factory, and today it is the most popular grout of all. It made cement better, and did not pretend otherwise.

16. Furan resin. From the nineteen-forties, a grout of great chemical resistance, trusted in dairies, in chemical plants, and in some pools. Furan grouts are generally black. The museum regrets that its specimen is not.

17. Epoxy. Its emulsions came in the nineteen-forties; true epoxy grouts were in general use by the sixties and seventies. Class R G, under B S E N thirteen eight eight eight: the pool's choice where the water is soft, or aggressive. Some are rated even for the sea. It is what the water deserves.

18. Today. Class C G two W A: improved cement, absorbing less water, resisting the scrub of brush and foot, often guarding itself against mould. The old way, made as good as it can be.

19. And finally, a confession. This is not grout. It is a movement joint: a flexible sealant where the pool must be allowed to move, at its edges and over its structure's own joints. Not grout. But grout could not do its work without it.

20. Thank you for visiting. As you go back out into the world, and into the water, spare a thought for the joints between the tiles. They never ask to be noticed. That is why we notice them.

HALL 8: NEVER AGAIN, PUTTING SAFETY FIRST

21. Pellingford Baths, a spring morning in 1997. At seventeen minutes to eight a chemical tanker pulls into the loading bay. It is early. The duty technician has not yet started his shift, and the driver has never been here before. Nobody comes out to meet him.

22. Two fill points stand side by side in an open recess: one for acid, one for sodium hypochlorite. They have the same coupling, and their labels faded long ago. The driver connects his acid hose to the hypochlorite fill. Four hundred litres of acid go into the bulk hypochlorite tank.

23. Mixed, acid and hypochlorite give off chlorine: a choking, yellow-green gas, heavier than air. It rises from the tank's vent into the plant room, and the air handling draws it towards the pool hall. The plant room's gas detector should have raised the alarm. It had been disconnected, weeks before, after false alarms.

24. A lifeguard clears the pool. Swimmers and staff stand in the car park in foil blankets while fire crews in gas-tight suits go in. A handful are treated for breathing difficulties. Nobody dies. But there was no plan, and nobody had practised one.

25. Weeks later, the inspectors report. There was no risk assessment for deliveries. The staff had not been trained to handle the chemicals. Nobody had checked the contractor. The council is fined forty thousand pounds, and the baths are shut for six weeks.

26. Pellingford Baths reopened with its fill points rebuilt: a different coupling for each chemical, each in its own locked cabinet, clearly labelled. Every delivery is met. The gas detector works, and the stores are apart. The fix is to make the mistake impossible. Never again.
