## Standards

- **BS EN 13888** classes grouts: **CG1** normal cementitious grout; **CG2** cementitious grout with improved characteristics; additional suffixes **W** (reduced water absorption) and **A** (high abrasion resistance); **RG** reaction-resin grout [S1, S3]. A typical modern product is "CG2WA" [S3]. (Since 2017 the standard is EN 13888 itself; earlier editions date from 2002 and 2009, **indicative**.)
- **BS 5385-4** covers tiling in specific conditions including swimming pools; manufacturers cite it for the rule that a pool is not filled for at least three weeks after grouting [S3, S8]. BS 5385 guidance quoted by ARDEX: joints about 2 mm on walls (typically mosaic), 3 mm on floors, 5 mm for large format; movement joints to BS 5385-4 [S2]. BS 5385-3 covers floors generally (**indicative** as to its pool content; the pool guidance is in Part 4).

## Pools and grout

- ARDEX: cementitious CG2WA grouts suit pools with balanced hard water and ancillary areas; epoxy (reaction-resin) grouts are recommended for aggressive or soft water and high-abrasion zones, being impervious and chemically resistant [S2].
- Epoxy RG grouts are sold for pools, some explicitly for seawater and spa water [S7].
- Cement grout is calcium-based. Water low in calcium hardness, or with a negative saturation (Langelier) index, dissolves calcium compounds from cementitious surfaces, so grout is leached, roughened and lost from the joints [S9; trade forum and maker statements, **indicative** in detail]. A saturation index of -0.3 or below trends corrosive [S9]; 0 to +0.3 is satisfactory [S10]; calcium hardness for tiled pools is commonly kept at 200 to 400 ppm [S9] (UK PWTAG figures not checked here, **indicative**).
- Acid cleaners (used to remove scale and cement haze) also attack cement grout; epoxy resists them [S6, S2].

## History, kind by kind

- **Lime.** Lime mortars and lime putty were the jointing materials of masonry and tiling before Portland cement was common; soft, slow to carbonate, soluble in soft or acid water [general building history, **indicative**]. Victorian glazed tiling and faience were typically set with fine joints, not thick ones (**indicative**).
- **Portland cement.** Aspdin's patent is 1824; the hotter-burned material resembling modern Portland cement dates from the 1840s, and it spread through the later nineteenth century [S11, **indicative**]. Neat cement or cement-and-fine-sand grouts became the standard joint filler for tiling [general, **indicative**].
- **Sanded and unsanded cement grouts.** The twentieth-century split by joint width: sand for wider joints to resist shrinkage cracking, unsanded for fine joints [S4, general trade practice; the ~3 mm threshold is a trade rule of thumb, **indicative**].
- **Latex and polymer modification.** Installers long added a latex admixture in place of water for a denser, less absorbent grout; factory-premixed polymer-modified cement grouts are now the most popular type [S4]. Dates (1960s onward) **indicative**.
- **Furan and epoxy.** Furan grouts and epoxy emulsion grouts were introduced in the 1940s; furan for chemical resistance [S5]. Furan resin grouts are typically black (carbon-filled) (**indicative**, widely stated by makers). True 100%-solids epoxy grouts were in general use by the 1960s and 70s (**indicative**). Epoxy: higher chemical resistance, more cost, two-part mixing, short pot life, operator hazard [S6, patent background].
- **Coloured cement grouts.** Pigmented grouts were sold to match coloured bathroom suites; avocado was a defining UK suite colour of the 1970s (Armitage Shanks, Twyfords and others, **indicative**). Pigmented cement grout can fade or set patchy ("efflorescence and shade variation", **indicative**).
- **Modern cement grouts.** CG2WA grouts with water repellence and mould inhibitors, some rapid-setting [S3].
- **Movement joints.** Flexible sealants (silicone, polysulphide, polyurethane) at perimeters, changes of plane and over structural joints, to BS 5385 [S2].

## The joint-length fact

A 25 × 12.5 m pool, 1.5 m deep throughout: floor 312.5 m² plus walls 2 × (25 + 12.5) × 1.5 = 112.5 m², 425 m² in all. A square metre of 150 mm tiles has 2 / 0.15 = 13.3 m of joint. 425 × 13.3 ≈ 5,670 m: about 5.7 km of grout joint in the tank alone. (Computed; it ignores real pools' deeper ends and the deck.)
