# Pool water chemistry and biology: models to borrow

Researched 2026-10-22 for the owner's request for a richer water model, in which plant failures can make the water go profoundly wrong (algae, bacterial growth, cloudiness, chloramine build-up), while staying simple to simulate. It answers four questions: what published models exist for pool chemistry, what exists for microbial and algal growth, what open-source code could be reused, and what each plant failure does to the water and how fast. UK rules apply throughout (PWTAG Code of Practice, HSG282 for the jacuzzi, BS EN 15288).

Conventions: statements carry their source as a link. _(inferred)_ marks my reasoning from the sources; _(derived)_ marks arithmetic I did from sourced numbers (stoichiometry, unit conversion); _(invented)_ marks a number the game would have to make up because I found nothing citable. Where a source was read only through a search-engine summary rather than in full, it says so. The current code is described from `src/game/plant/pools.ts` and `src/game/plant/chemicals.ts` as they stand; nothing was changed.

## 1. Summary and recommendation

**No-one has published a ready-made "pool going wrong" simulator.** I found no game, teaching simulation or open-source pool simulator that couples chlorine chemistry with bacteria and algae. The pool-maths apps (TroubleFreePool's PoolMath and its open-source cousins) are dosing calculators for a static water sample, not time-stepped models, and are aimed at American residential pools with cyanuric acid. What does exist is a set of well-characterised pieces from water-treatment science, and they bolt together cleanly.

**Recommended pieces to adopt:**

1. **Chlorine speciation and its temperature dependence**: the textbook HOCl/OCl⁻ equilibrium with the temperature polynomials used in the US EPA's MIT-licensed Chlorine Breakpoint Curve Simulator (§3.1). Exact, cheap, sourced.
2. **Chloramine chemistry**: the Jafvert & Valentine (1992) reaction scheme, with Vikesland et al. (2001) refinements, exactly as coded in the EPA simulator (`USEPA/Chlorine_Breakpoint_Curve_Simulator`, MIT). It is the same backbone PWTAG's own commissioned research at Bristol used for pool modelling, with a first-order chlorine-loss term and a urea term added (Davis & Bullock, 2002). The full scheme is stiff (rate constants up to 5.6 × 10¹⁰), so for the game I recommend a **four-variable reduced form** (free chlorine, combined chlorine, ammoniacal nitrogen, urea) calibrated against it off-line (§3.3, §6).
3. **Bather load**: Judd & Bullock's 50 ml urine and 200 ml sweat per bather, Keuten's ~80 mg nitrogen per bather, and a per-bather chlorine demand of 2.8 to 11 g Cl₂ (Judd & Black; Berg et al.). Together with PWTAG's bathing-load and dilution rules (30 litres of fresh water per bather), these drive everything else (§3.2).
4. **pH**: a carbonate-alkalinity state with Wojtowicz's measured carbon-dioxide outgassing kinetics (the main reason pool pH rises, more than the hypochlorite itself), and the TroubleFreePool Calcium Saturation Index formula (implemented under MIT in `pypoolchem`) for scale and corrosion (§3.4).
5. **Microbes**: one equation per organism, logistic growth with a cardinal-temperature curve (Rosso et al., 1993) minus Chick–Watson kill proportional to hypochlorous acid, minus removal by filtration and UV, plus inputs from bathers and from biofilm reservoirs in the balance tank and filters. Kill constants come from published CT values (CDC, PWTAG, WHO-derived figures); growth rates for pool conditions mostly have to be invented within sourced bounds (§4).
6. **Failure outcomes and thresholds**: straight from the PWTAG Code of Practice (July 2021) and HSE HSG282, which give numeric closure criteria (§7, §8).

**Key honest gap:** ORP has no clean published relationship to free chlorine that holds across probes; every source fits its own curve. The game should use a Nernst-shaped curve in log(HOCl) calibrated to the German DIN 19643 operating anchors (750 to 770 mV), and say so (§3.5). Algae growth in chlorinated pool water has no usable published kinetics at all; the game must invent it (§9).

## 3. Chemistry

### 3.1 Free chlorine: hypochlorous acid against hypochlorite

Free chlorine is HOCl plus OCl⁻; HOCl does nearly all the disinfecting ([Pickens, "Relative Effects of pH and Cyanurate on Disinfection"](https://www.poolhelp.com/wp-content/uploads/2017/11/Relative-Effects-of-pH-and-Cyanurate-on-Disinfection.pdf) cites HOCl as about 100 times as effective as OCl⁻). The fraction present as HOCl is

`α = 1 / (1 + 10^(pH − pKa))`

with pKa about 7.4 to 7.5 depending on temperature and ionic strength (Pickens: 7.44 at 27 °C, ionic strength 0.04). The EPA simulator gives the temperature dependence as ([EPA CBCS `app.R`](https://github.com/USEPA/Chlorine_Breakpoint_Curve_Simulator)):

`pKa(T) = 1.18×10⁻⁴·T² − 7.86×10⁻²·T + 20.5` (T in kelvin)

which agrees within 0.02 with Morris (1966), `pKa = 3000/T − 10.0686 + 0.0253·T` ([Morris, J. Phys. Chem. 70, 3798, cited via a search summary of a poolhelp.com reprint](https://www.poolhelp.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/09/6-2-Reevaluation-of-Constants.pdf)). Values _(derived)_:

| Water °C     | pKa  | α at pH 7.0 | 7.2  | 7.4  | 7.6  | 7.8  | 8.0  | 8.2  |
| ------------ | ---- | ----------- | ---- | ---- | ---- | ---- | ---- | ---- |
| 28 (Olympic) | 7.53 | 0.77        | 0.68 | 0.58 | 0.46 | 0.35 | 0.25 | 0.18 |
| 32 (lounge)  | 7.50 | 0.76        | 0.67 | 0.56 | 0.44 | 0.34 | 0.24 | 0.17 |
| 37 (jacuzzi) | 7.47 | 0.75        | 0.65 | 0.54 | 0.43 | 0.32 | 0.23 | 0.16 |

So a pool drifting from pH 7.4 to 8.0 loses well over half its disinfecting power at the same free-chlorine reading. That is why PWTAG asks for 2.0 mg/l free chlorine at pH 7.2 to 7.4 but only about 1 mg/l at pH 7.0 to 7.2 ([PWTAG CoP 2021 §9.5, §9.8](https://www.gov.gg/CHttpHandler.ashx?id=191649&p=0)).

**Cyanuric acid.** UK public pools on sodium hypochlorite do not use it. The CoP allows chloroisocyanurates only with free chlorine at 5 mg/l and cyanuric acid no more than 100 mg/l ([CoP §9.6](https://www.gov.gg/CHttpHandler.ashx?id=191649&p=0)). Pickens shows that even 12.5 mg/l of cyanuric acid cuts HOCl more than raising the pH from 7.0 to 8.6. For the game: leave cyanuric acid out, or keep it only as a failure (a wrong delivery of stabilised chlorine) _(inferred)_. HSG282 and CDC both advise against stabilised chlorine in spas ([CDC position via an Aqua Magazine summary](https://www.aquamagazine.com/service/article/15122983/cyafree-sanitizing-solutions-for-hot-tubs)).

### 3.2 Bather load and chlorine demand

These are the inputs that make the water go wrong when the plant cannot keep up.

- **Body fluids.** "50 mL of urine and 200 mL of sweat are assumed to be released per bather on average" ([Judd & Bullock 2003, Chemosphere 51:869, quoted in Müller et al., SPSR](https://www.swimming.org/library/documents/3963/download)).
- **Nitrogen.** Average release during 30 minutes of exercise: 250 mg/bather non-purgeable organic carbon, 77.3 mg/bather total nitrogen, 37.1 mg/bather urea and 10.1 mg/bather ammonium ([Keuten et al., TU Delft repository, via search summary](https://repository.tudelft.nl/file/File_a9a0cfb5-3769-4679-920d-f5fc9154d759?preview=1)). Keuten's 2012 Water Research paper puts the _initial_ (pre-swim, unshowered) release at about 305 mg TOC and 80 mg total nitrogen per bather (same summary). Both depend heavily on showering and activity; "results … are very inconsistent" ([Müller et al.](https://www.swimming.org/library/documents/3963/download)).
- **Body-fluid analogue (BFA).** The Bristol PWTAG work used urea 7.4 g/l, ammonium chloride 2 g/l, hippuric acid 0.85, histidine 0.6, uric acid 0.245 g/l, and found that modelling only urea and ammonium chloride "did not significantly alter" the dynamics ([Davis & Bullock 2002, Table 1 and §3.1](https://www.swimming.org/library/documents/3893/download)). That justifies a game model with just two nitrogen pools.
- **Chlorine demand per bather.** 2,800 mg Cl₂/bather in bench trials with body-fluid analogues (Judd & Black 2000, Water Res. 34:1611, as reported by Berg et al.); 4,120 and 11,030 mg Cl₂/bather fitted to two real indoor pools, one lightly loaded (0.038 bathers/m³/day) and one heavily (0.36 bathers/m³/day) ([Berg, Fang & Tang 2019, J. Water Health](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10086463)). Berg's model is just `ΔCl₂ = a − k·n` (dose minus k times bathers), with R² > 0.97. A French study gives about 7.0 g Cl₂ per "equivalent bather" exerted in the first hour and 10.5 g in 24 hours ([AIVC, via search summary](https://www.aivc.org/sites/default/files/airbase_12465.pdf)): roughly two thirds of the demand is exerted within an hour _(derived)_.
- **Dilution.** PWTAG: replace pool water with fresh water "at a rate of 30 litres per bather"; backwash water counts towards it ([CoP §3.6](https://www.gov.gg/CHttpHandler.ashx?id=191649&p=0)).
- **Bathing load.** Maximum bathing load 1 bather per 2.2 m² (<1.0 m deep), 2.7 m² (1.0 to 1.5 m), 4.0 m² (>1.5 m); operational daily load = 25 to 50 % of maximum × hours open; circulation rate (m³/h) = maximum bathing load × 1.7 ([CoP §3.7 to §3.8](https://www.gov.gg/CHttpHandler.ashx?id=191649&p=0)).

**What that means for our pools** _(derived, using the pool sizes in `src/game/plant-sizing/pools.config.ts` and 14 hours open)_: the lounge pool (139 m³, 154 m², under 1 m deep) has a maximum of about 70 bathers and an operational load of 245 to 490 a day; at 4 g Cl₂ each that is 1 to 2 kg Cl₂ a day, or 7 to 14 mg/l a day. The Olympic pool (2,500 m³, 1,250 m²) has about 310 maximum and 1,100 to 2,200 a day: 1.8 to 3.5 mg/l a day. The jacuzzi (2.83 m³) is the extreme: even 50 bathers a day at 4 g is about 70 mg/l a day, which is why spas need 3 to 5 mg/l and a 6-minute turnover. The game's present dose, 60 ml of 14 % hypochlorite per m³ per day, supplies about 8.4 mg/l a day _(derived: 0.06 l × ~140 g/l available chlorine)_: about right for the lounge, generous for the Olympic pool and far short for a busy jacuzzi.

### 3.3 Chloramines and breakpoint chlorination

**The full model.** The EPA simulator ([`app.R`](https://github.com/USEPA/Chlorine_Breakpoint_Curve_Simulator), after [Jafvert & Valentine 1992, ES&T 26:577](http://pubs.acs.org/doi/abs/10.1021/es00027a022) and [Vikesland et al. 2001, Water Res. 35:1766](http://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0043135400004061)) tracks total free chlorine (TOTCl), total ammonia (TOTNH), NH₂Cl, NHCl₂, NCl₃ and an intermediate I. Rate constants, mol/l and seconds, T in kelvin (values at 25 °C in brackets, as commented in the source):

| #   | Reaction                               | Rate constant                                                                           |
| --- | -------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1   | HOCl + NH₃ → NH₂Cl + H₂O               | 6.6×10⁸·e^(−1510/T) (4.2×10⁶ M⁻¹s⁻¹)                                                    |
| 2   | NH₂Cl + H₂O → HOCl + NH₃               | 1.38×10⁸·e^(−8800/T) (2.1×10⁻⁵ s⁻¹)                                                     |
| 3   | HOCl + NH₂Cl → NHCl₂ + H₂O             | 3.0×10⁵·e^(−2010/T) (2.8×10² M⁻¹s⁻¹)                                                    |
| 4   | NHCl₂ + H₂O → NH₂Cl + HOCl             | 6.5×10⁻⁷ s⁻¹                                                                            |
| 5   | 2 NH₂Cl → NHCl₂ + NH₃ (acid-catalysed) | k5 = 1.05×10⁷e^(−2169/T)[H⁺] + 4.2×10³¹e^(−22144/T)[HCO₃⁻] + 8.19×10⁶e^(−4026/T)[H₂CO₃] |
| 6   | NHCl₂ + NH₃ + H⁺ → 2 NH₂Cl             | 6.0×10⁴ M⁻²s⁻¹                                                                          |
| 7   | NHCl₂ + OH⁻ → I                        | 1.1×10² M⁻¹s⁻¹                                                                          |
| 8   | I + NHCl₂ → HOCl + products            | 2.8×10⁴                                                                                 |
| 9   | I + NH₂Cl → products                   | 8.3×10³                                                                                 |
| 10  | NH₂Cl + NHCl₂ → products               | 1.5×10⁻²                                                                                |
| 11  | HOCl + NHCl₂ → NCl₃ + H₂O              | 3.28×10⁹[OH⁻] + 6.0×10⁶[CO₃²⁻], plus 9×10⁴[OCl⁻]                                        |
| 12  | NHCl₂ + NCl₃ + 2OH⁻ → 2HOCl + products | 5.56×10¹⁰                                                                               |
| 13  | NH₂Cl + NCl₃ + OH⁻ → products          | 1.39×10⁹                                                                                |
| 14  | NHCl₂ + 2HOCl → products (breakpoint)  | 2.31×10²                                                                                |

Equilibrium constants in the same source: pKa(HOCl) as in §3.1; `pKa(NH₄⁺) = 1.03×10⁻⁴T² − 9.21×10⁻²T + 27.6` (≈9.25); `pK1(H₂CO₃) = 1.48×10⁻⁴T² − 9.39×10⁻²T + 21.2` (≈6.35); `pK2(HCO₃⁻) = 1.19×10⁻⁴T² − 7.99×10⁻²T + 23.6` (≈10.33); `pKw = 1.5×10⁻⁴T² − 1.23×10⁻¹T + 37.3`. Total carbonate from alkalinity: `C_T = (Alk/50000 + [H⁺] − [OH⁻]) / (α₁ + 2α₂)`.

**Pool adaptations (Bristol, for PWTAG).** Davis & Bullock verified the scheme against closed-bottle experiments and added (a) a first-order chlorine loss `k_loss·[HOCl]` and (b) a urea sink `k_urea·[urea][HOCl]` with **k_urea = 778.8 M⁻¹h⁻¹**, urea forming chloroureas and consuming chlorine without making much measurable chloramine. They found the scheme over-predicts trichloramine at high pH and under-predicts dichloramine, and that the DPD test misreads chlorinated organics as chloramines ([Davis & Bullock 2002, §1.2, §2.2.2, §4.3](https://www.swimming.org/library/documents/3893/download)). In a 2.2 m³ model pool, organic carbon, chloramines and THMs all reached steady state after 200 to 500 hours, and nitrate was the only by-product that kept building up ([Judd & Bullock 2003, via abstract summaries](https://pherobase.com/literature/data/details/A/13783)).

**Urea is slow; trichloramine is what the hall smells of.** Under pool conditions urea half-lives exceed 23 hours, and urea gives a relative NCl₃ yield of 76 % at pH 7.1; NCl₃ transfers to the air over 20 hours (rough surface) to 5.8 days (quiescent) ([Schmalz et al. 2011, Water Res. 45:2681, via search summary](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21420139/)). With the Bristol constant, urea's half-life at 1 mg/l free chlorine (all as HOCl) is about 63 hours _(derived: 778.8 M⁻¹h⁻¹ ÷ 70,900 mg/mol = 0.011 l/(mg·h))_, consistent with ">23 h".

**The game's reduced form** _(derived from the reaction stoichiometry; constants to be fitted against the EPA model and the Bristol data)_: ammonia meets free chlorine and becomes monochloramine almost instantly (reaction 1 at ~4×10⁶ M⁻¹s⁻¹ is a pseudo-first-order rate of tens per second at pool chlorine levels), so ammonium-N entering a chlorinated pool becomes combined chlorine at once, consuming 5.07 mg Cl₂ per mg N (71/14). Excess free chlorine then destroys combined chlorine over hours (breakpoint, 2NH₂Cl + HOCl → N₂ + 3HCl + H₂O), consuming a further 0.5 mg free chlorine per mg combined chlorine destroyed: 7.6 mg Cl₂ per mg N in all, the classic breakpoint ratio (3 × 71 / 2 × 14). Counting the hypochlorite as dosed (OCl⁻ takes up a proton as it becomes HOCl), the overall reaction 2 NH₄⁺ + 3 OCl⁻ → N₂ + 3 Cl⁻ + 3 H₂O + 2 H⁺ releases one equivalent of acid per mole of N: about 3.6 mg of alkalinity as CaCO₃ per mg N oxidised (50/14), a small offset to the pH rise. Nitrogen in a pool with no free chlorine stays as ammonia and forms no chloramine: combined chlorine _falls_ (by dilution and slow decay) if the hypochlorite runs out, while the ammonia waits for chlorine to return _(inferred from the scheme)_.

**Removal.** UV (medium-pressure) breaks chloramines down; one French study reports 67 % reduction to below 0.2 mg/l at 145 mJ/cm² ([via a Counsilman-Hunsaker summary](https://counsilmanhunsaker.com/ultraviolet-light-uv/); vendor claims of 80 % are unverified). DTU used UV removal of combined chlorine as an actinometer, i.e. it is first order in dose ([Spiliotopoulou et al., DTU](https://orbit.dtu.dk/en/publications/photolytic-removal-of-dbps-by-medium-pressure-uv-in-swimming-pool-2/)). PWTAG: secondary disinfection reduces "troublesome, irritant chloramines" and allows lower residuals ([CoP §3.5](https://www.gov.gg/CHttpHandler.ashx?id=191649&p=0)). Dilution removes what UV does not.

**Limits.** Combined chlorine "should always be less than half the free chlorine, and no more than 1 mg/l no matter what the level of free chlorine" ([CoP §9.7](https://www.gov.gg/CHttpHandler.ashx?id=191649&p=0)). HSG282 says the same for spas and adds that above it "irritation to the mucous membranes of the eyes and throat may occur" ([HSG282 ¶136](https://www.hse.gov.uk/pubns/priced/hsg282.pdf)). Automatic controllers do not measure combined chlorine; staff find it by DPD 1 and DPD 3 tests ([CoP §9.2](https://www.gov.gg/CHttpHandler.ashx?id=191649&p=0)), so a chloramine build-up is something the player discovers by testing, not from an instrument _(inferred)_.

### 3.4 pH, alkalinity and scale

**Why pool pH rises.** Pool water is supersaturated with CO₂ and keeps losing it, so pH drifts upward; "the major portion of added acid is needed to restore pH due to carbon dioxide loss". Hypochlorite "will augment the pH drift, but only to a small extent", and oxidation of organics only slightly offsets it. CO₂ loss raises pH without changing alkalinity ([Wojtowicz, "Factors Affecting Loss of Carbon Dioxide", JSPSI 1(3):19, 2001](https://poolhelp.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/09/4-3-Factors-Affecting-Loss-of-CO2.pdf)). Whether hypochlorite is net pH-neutral is argued both ways in the trade press; the excess sodium hydroxide in the product makes it slightly alkaline, and its consumption releases acid ([forum and trade summaries](https://www.troublefreepool.com/threads/swg-ph-neutral.8562), [Pool & Spa Marketing](https://poolspamarketing.com/trade/features/expert-advice/an-intermediate-look-at-water-chemistry-and-testing/2)). For the game, Wojtowicz's measured verdict is the one to follow _(inferred)_.

**Wojtowicz's kinetics** (same source): CO₂ loss is second order in excess dissolved CO₂, `dC/dt = k·(C_ex − C)²`, with k in (ppm·day)⁻¹ almost independent of alkalinity. In a 6,800-gallon test pool at 80 to 84 °F:

| Pump duty          | Carbonate alkalinity, ppm | pH start → end | k, (ppm·day)⁻¹ |
| ------------------ | ------------------------- | -------------- | -------------- |
| 24 h               | 54                        | 7.33 → 7.82    | 0.19           |
| 24 h               | 79                        | 7.39 → 8.03    | 0.21           |
| 24 h               | 96                        | 7.33 → 8.03    | 0.17           |
| 24 h, pool covered | 83                        | 7.35 → 7.51    | 0.016          |
| 8 h                | 73                        | 7.34 → 7.70    | 0.066          |
| 0 h                | 67                        | 7.35 → 7.50    | 0.034          |

Test runs were typically 7 days. He fits `k = 0.0341 + 0.009·TR + 0.0016·TR²`, with TR the turnovers per day, scaled in proportion to surface-to-volume ratio and by Arrhenius with ΔE = 5.0 kcal/mol; a cover cuts k tenfold; the heater has little effect. The equilibrium dissolved CO₂ is only about 0.45 ppm at 82 °F. The Henry's-law constant he quotes is Plummer & Busenberg's, `log K_H = 108.3865 + 0.01985076·T − 6919.53/T − 40.45154·log T + 669365/T²` (the leading sign is garbled in the PDF text; this sign gives log K_H ≈ −1.47 at 25 °C, the textbook value) _(derived)_. My carbonate calculation with these constants gives about 6 to 12 mg/l of dissolved CO₂ at pH 7.4 for alkalinity 80 to 150, and an equilibrium pH with outdoor air of about 8.4 to 8.7: the pH a pool heads for if no acid is dosed _(derived)_. An indoor hall's air holds more CO₂ from the bathers, so the ceiling there will be a little lower _(inferred)_. Public pools turn over every 2.5 hours or less ([CoP Table 2](https://www.gov.gg/CHttpHandler.ashx?id=191649&p=0)) and have water features and jets, so their k is larger than Wojtowicz's residential pool _(inferred)_.

**Alkalinity and hardness.** PWTAG: total alkalinity 80 to 200 mg/l as CaCO₃ "to ensure effective coagulation and a stable pH"; calcium hardness ideally 80 to 200 mg/l, and above 300 mg/l scale may deposit with sudden changes in temperature and pH ([CoP §9.9, §9.10](https://www.gov.gg/CHttpHandler.ashx?id=191649&p=0)). pH 7.0 to 7.4 for chlorine ([CoP §9.8](https://www.gov.gg/CHttpHandler.ashx?id=191649&p=0)).

**Scale and corrosion index.** The TroubleFreePool Calcium Saturation Index, as implemented under MIT in [pypoolchem `csi.py`](https://github.com/joyfulhouse/pypoolchem):

`CSI = pH − 11.677 + log₁₀(CH) + log₁₀(CarbAlk) − 2.56·√I / (1 + 1.65·√I) − 1412.5/(T + 273.15) + 4.7375`

with `I = (1.5·CH + TA)/50045 + extraNaCl/58440` and `CarbAlk = TA − 0.38772·CYA/(1 + 10^(6.83 − pH))` (zero cyanuric acid for us). Bands: ≤ −0.6 corrosive (etches grout), −0.3 to +0.3 balanced, ≥ +0.6 scale-forming (and cloudy water from calcium carbonate precipitation, which Wojtowicz links to CO₂ loss). That gives a sourced mechanism for "the pH ran away and the water went milky" in hard-water areas _(inferred)_.

### 3.5 Redox (ORP)

**Theory.** ORP responds mainly to HOCl, whose standard potential (1.48 V) is far above that of OCl⁻ (0.81 V) ([US patent 8,999,139](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/8999139)). For HOCl + H⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cl⁻ + H₂O the Nernst slope is 29.6 mV per decade of HOCl, and the potential depends on chloride and pH as much as on HOCl ([Emerson/Rosemount ORP application data sheet, via search summary](https://d1-auth.emerson.com/documents/automation/application-data-sheet-fundamentals-of-orp-measurement-rosemount-en-68438.pdf)). Real probes do not follow Nernst cleanly; slopes differ between makers ([TroubleFreePool threads, forum source](https://www.troublefreepool.com/threads/126776-ORP-stuff)). One forum fit for a particular controller is `ORP ≈ 641 + 38.5·ln(FC)` mV, about 27 mV per doubling or 89 mV per decade ([TroubleFreePool, forum source, via search summary](https://www.troublefreepool.com/goto/post?id=2389517)). ORP also falls as organic load rises: at a German pool under heavy load it dipped to about 800 mV with free chlorine 0.34 to 0.69 mg/l ([Müller et al.](https://www.swimming.org/library/documents/3963/download)).

**Anchors.** DIN 19643 (older edition): at least 750 mV at pH 6.5 to 7.3 and 770 mV at pH 7.3 to 7.5, against a silver/silver chloride reference ([Munich Merkblatt, via search summary](https://stadt.muenchen.de/dam/jcr:56d93c99-57ca-4695-b4a1-d9d6623890e2/merkblatt_baeder_parameter.pdf)); the 2023 edition gives an operating display of 700 to 770 mV ([LAGuS Mecklenburg-Vorpommern, via search summary](https://www.lagus.mv-regierung.de/serviceassistent/download?id=1665940)). The often-quoted "650 mV kills E. coli in 30 seconds (WHO 1972)" appears only in patent background text and is disputed ([patent text](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/10836661), [forum](https://www.troublefreepool.com/threads/ideal-orp-readings.30907)). PWTAG treats redox as inferential and wants chlorine controlled amperometrically ([TN39, summarised in `water-sampling.md`](https://www.pwtag.org/residual-control-september-2018/)).

**Recommendation** _(invented, sourced shape)_: `ORP = E₀ + S·log₁₀(HOCl + h₀) − c·D_org`, HOCl in mg/l. With S = 80 mV/decade, E₀ = 786 mV and h₀ = 10⁻⁴ mg/l the curve passes 750 mV at 0.35 mg/l HOCl (DIN's minimum at about 0.5 mg/l free chlorine, pH 7), gives about 777 mV at our nominal 1.4 mg/l and pH 7.4, about 660 mV at 0.1 mg/l and pH 8, and bottoms out near 470 mV with no chlorine. `D_org` is a small optional term for organic load. pH enters through α, which is the real mechanism, rather than as a separate linear term.

### 3.6 Temperature

Temperature lowers pKa slightly (§3.1), speeds every reaction (Arrhenius terms in §3.3, Wojtowicz's 5 kcal/mol for CO₂), raises chlorine demand and evaporation, and moves microbes into their growth range: HSG282 says spa water is "usually maintained within the temperature range where legionellae and other infectious microorganisms can rapidly grow (20–45 °C)" ([HSG282](https://www.hse.gov.uk/pubns/priced/hsg282.pdf)). The typical spa operating temperature is 30 to 40 °C ([HSG282 ¶102](https://www.hse.gov.uk/pubns/priced/hsg282.pdf)).

### 4.2 Growth

**Shape.** Logistic growth `dN/dt = μ(T)·N·(1 − N/K)` with μ from the cardinal temperature model with inflection ([Rosso, Lobry & Flandrois 1993, J. Theor. Biol. 162:447, equation via MetricGate](https://metricgate.com/docs/cardinal-temperature-model-ctmi/)):

`μ(T) = μ_opt·(T − T_max)(T − T_min)² / ((T_opt − T_min)·[(T_opt − T_min)(T − T_opt) − (T_opt − T_max)(T_opt + T_min − 2T)])`, zero outside T_min to T_max.

A Monod nutrient factor `S/(K_s + S)` (phosphate for algae, organic carbon for bacteria) can multiply μ; I found no K_s values for pool conditions, so it is _(invented)_.

### 4.3 Biofilm: the reservoir that makes failures recur

PWTAG: P. aeruginosa "can easily colonise water systems, forming biofilms and their presence requires cleaning and disinfection with a 50 mg/l free chlorine solution" ([CoP §10.4](https://www.gov.gg/CHttpHandler.ashx?id=191649&p=0)). HSG282 prescribes scrubbing the balance tank, cleansing the filter, and shock dosing at 50 mg/l for an hour when Pseudomonas, coliforms or Legionella exceed limits ([HSG282 Table 4](https://www.hse.gov.uk/pubns/priced/hsg282.pdf)). For the game, give each circuit two or three biofilm reservoirs (balance tank, filter bed, deck channel), each a 0 to 1 "coverage" that grows slowly whenever free chlorine there is below about 0.3 mg/l, sheds organisms into the water in proportion to its coverage, and is cleared only by a shock dose or cleaning, not by restoring normal chlorine _(invented mechanism, sourced behaviour)_. This gives the realistic pattern of repeat positive samples after the chlorine is back in range.

### 4.4 Removal by filtration and UV

- **Filtration.** Medium-rate sand filters with continuous coagulation remove "some 99% of the Cryptosporidium oocysts in each pass"; high-rate filters without coagulation "as little as 10%", with coagulation perhaps 50 % ([CoP, faecal-incident section](https://www.gov.gg/CHttpHandler.ashx?id=191649&p=0)). An effective system removes more than 90 % of oocysts per pass ([CoP §5](https://www.gov.gg/CHttpHandler.ashx?id=191649&p=0)). Because filtered water mixes back into the pool, it may take as long as 24 hours for all the water to pass through the filters at a 3 to 4-hour turnover (same section); in a well-mixed model the concentration falls as `exp(−η·Q·t/V)` _(derived)_.
- **UV and ozone.** Designed for 99 % Cryptosporidium reduction per pass ([CoP §3.5](https://www.gov.gg/CHttpHandler.ashx?id=191649&p=0)). UV "does not oxidise", so it leaves no residual ([CoP §6.7](https://www.gov.gg/CHttpHandler.ashx?id=191649&p=0)).
- **Dosing interlock.** "Dosing pumps should be designed to shut themselves off if the circulation system fails (though automatic water quality monitors should remain in operation)" ([CoP §3.4](https://www.gov.gg/CHttpHandler.ashx?id=191649&p=0)). The game already does this.

## 5. Cloudiness

PWTAG's maximum turbidity is 0.5 NTU, and "It should be possible to see clearly the detail of the bottom of the pool at its deepest point"; poor clarity is an immediate drowning risk and compromises disinfection ([CoP §5](https://www.gov.gg/CHttpHandler.ashx?id=191649&p=0)). Causes the game can model: bather particles (particle counts track bather numbers closely and accumulate when input exceeds removal; turbidity peaked at 0.5 FNU the day after peak load even with ultrafiltration ([Müller et al.](https://www.swimming.org/library/documents/3963/download))); a dirty or channelling filter; coagulant not dosed; calcium carbonate precipitating when CSI is high (§3.4); algae (green water) and bacterial growth. A simple particle balance is enough: `dP/dt = inputs − (Q/V)·η_filter·P`, turbidity ∝ P plus algae and scale terms _(invented scaling)_.

## 6. A proposed game model

Per pool, all well mixed, stepped every few seconds of game time as now (all concentrations mg/l):

**State.** FC (free chlorine), CC (combined chlorine), NH (ammoniacal N), U (urea N), D (other organic chlorine demand, as mg/l Cl₂ equivalent), TA (alkalinity as CaCO₃), CO₂ex (excess CO₂), CH (calcium hardness), T, P (particles, for turbidity), log₁₀ counts for ACC, E. coli, coliforms, P. aeruginosa, Legionella (jacuzzi only), Cryptosporidium, algae (green, mustard, black), and biofilm coverage per site. pH is computed from TA, CO₂ex and T by the carbonate equilibria (§3.3 constants), not stepped directly.

**Inputs per bather** (spread over their visit): 10 mg ammonium (≈ 8 mg N) to NH, ~30 mg N as urea to U, the balance of a 2.8 to 4 g Cl₂ demand to D (less the ~0.6 g that the nitrogen itself will take, at 7.6 mg Cl₂ per mg N), particles, a small E. coli and coliform dose, occasional P. aeruginosa _(input sizes partly invented)_. A faecal incident is a large one-off dose of E. coli, and with diarrhoea, Cryptosporidium.

**Equations** _(invented assembly of sourced parts)_:

- `α = 1/(1 + 10^(pH − pKa(T)))`; HOCl = α·FC.
- Ammonia to chloramine (fast): each step, `x = min(NH, FC/5.07)`; NH −= x; CC += 5.07x; FC −= 5.07x.
- Breakpoint: `r = k_bp·HOCl·CC`; CC −= r·dt; FC −= 0.5·r·dt; TA −= 0.7·r·dt (3.6 mg CaCO₃ per mg N ÷ 5.07 mg combined chlorine per mg N, _derived_ from §3.3). Fit k_bp so the reduced model reproduces the EPA scheme's breakpoint time at pool Cl:N ratios (hours).
- Urea: `U −= 0.011·HOCl·U·dt` (h⁻¹, Bristol), consuming chlorine and making some NCl₃ (a "chlorine smell" index for the hall) _(stoichiometry invented)_.
- Organic demand: `D −= k_D·HOCl·D·dt`, FC −= the same; k_D chosen so two thirds of a day's demand is exerted in the first hour (AIVC figures).
- Background chlorine loss: first order `k_loss·FC` (Bristol); indoors there is no sunlight term.
- UV: per pass, CC and microbes reduced by fractions (≈0.5 for CC, 0.99 for Crypto); ozone similar, plus a deozoniser.
- Dilution by make-up and backwash: every species relaxes towards mains values at rate (make-up flow)/V.
- CO₂ loss: `dCO₂ex/dt = −k_w·CO₂ex²`, k_w from Wojtowicz's turnover fit, higher for the jacuzzi and water features _(inferred)_. Acid dose: TA −, CO₂ex +. Hypochlorite: TA + a little (excess caustic, _invented_ size).
- Microbes: `d ln N/dt = μ(T)(1 − N/K) − 60·Λ_eff·HOCl − (Q/V)(η_filter + η_UV) − dilution` plus inputs (bathers, biofilm shedding) added linearly. Algae get μ × light × phosphate factor; light comes from the hall's daylight and lamps _(invented)_.
- Biofilm coverage: grows at a slow rate when FC < 0.3 mg/l at that site, sheds in proportion to coverage, cleared by FC ≥ 10 to 50 mg/l for an hour or by cleaning.
- Turbidity: from P, algae and a CaCO₃ term switched on when CSI > +0.6.
- ORP: §3.5.

**Integration.** Do not port the full 14-reaction scheme into the live game loop: it is stiff and needs an implicit solver with tolerances of 10⁻¹² (the EPA code uses R's `deSolve` with atol = rtol = 10⁻¹²). Port it instead into a test harness under `src/game/water/` and use it to fit k_bp and to test the reduced model _(inferred)_. All of the reduced equations are first or second order and can be stepped exactly or with exponential integrators, which keeps the state plain saveable data as the world-state rule requires.

**What the player sees.** Controllers show FC (amperometric), pH and redox; DPD tests in the plant room show FC, total and combined chlorine; weekly kits show alkalinity and hardness; turbidity is seen in the pool (and on an optional turbidimeter); microbiology arrives as a laboratory result days after the sample, exactly as in life ([CoP §10](https://www.gov.gg/CHttpHandler.ashx?id=191649&p=0)), so a bacterial problem is found late _(inferred)_.

## 9. What nobody has published, and must be invented

- **Microbial growth rates in pool water.** Everything found is for culture media or drinking-water bioreactors. Pool water is warm but chlorinated and lean; growth there is slower and mostly happens in biofilm. The game needs invented μ values, bounded above by the culture rates in §4.2, and a biofilm mechanism (§4.3) that has the documented behaviour but no published kinetics.
- **Algae kinetics under chlorine.** No CT values and no growth rates in pool water for green, mustard or black algae; only trade lore (chlorine above 1 mg/l prevents it; mustard and black algae resist). Light and phosphate dependence must be invented; Chlorella culture figures give an upper bound of about 1.7 per day.
- **P. aeruginosa kill constant.** No clean free-chlorine CT found; set it between E. coli and Giardia and make biofilm cells 10 to 100 times more resistant _(invented)_.
- **ORP.** Probe-specific; use the calibrated log-HOCl curve (§3.5).
- **Turbidity scaling.** Particles to NTU and algae to NTU are arbitrary; calibrate so that a failing filter takes days to cross 0.5 NTU and an algal bloom does it in a day or two.
- **The size of hypochlorite's caustic excess**, and **CO₂ loss in an indoor public pool** (Wojtowicz's constants are for a small outdoor residential pool; scale by turnover and surface-to-volume as he describes).
- **Exact chloramine reduction per UV pass** at our doses; vendor figures only.

## 10. How the current code compares

Not changes, just observations for whoever builds the new model:

- Free chlorine decays at a fixed 1.08 mg/l per hour whenever hypochlorite is absent, whatever the pool and its bathers (`-0.0003 * dt`); §3.2 suggests that is right for the lounge in use, far too fast for the Olympic pool and too slow for a busy jacuzzi, and near zero when no-one is bathing.
- Combined chlorine rises linearly without UV and falls with UV, independent of free chlorine and bathers; in the literature it forms only when chlorine and ammonia are both present, and it is destroyed by excess free chlorine (breakpoint) as well as by UV.
- Redox is linear in free chlorine (`620 + 72·FC − 45·(pH − 7.4)`); real redox is logarithmic in HOCl and gives about 777 mV at our nominal water rather than 720 mV (§3.5).
- pH: with hypochlorite only it approaches 8.2 with a time constant of under 3 hours; Wojtowicz's data suggest days, driven by CO₂ loss rather than the hypochlorite. With acid only it heads for 6.5; with closed-loop pH control that needs a stuck acid pump (§7), and an acid dose that had been balancing CO₂ loss would hold pH roughly level while alkalinity drained.
- Dosing is on or off per pool at a fixed daily rate; real controllers modulate dosing on the measured FC and pH, which is what makes "stuck on", "stuck off" and "probe fouled" distinct, playable failures _(inferred)_.
