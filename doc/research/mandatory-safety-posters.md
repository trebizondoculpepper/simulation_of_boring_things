# Mandatory safety posters, notices and signs

**Owner's decision (2026-10-22):** all signage in the game is modern, current UK practice (ISO 7010 signs, CLP pictograms, the 2009 HSE poster, the 2005 noise regulations' zones), to give the impression of an older facility in continuous use since the 1980s and partially upgraded. Which health and safety posters, notices and signs UK law requires in a workplace like ours (the staff-only plant areas of a municipal leisure centre, with its pools), which are only customary, and where each goes in our building. ## Short answer

Only a few notices are mandatory in themselves, in every workplace with employees: the HSE law poster (or the leaflet given to each employee instead), the employers' liability insurance certificate, and some way of telling staff the first-aid arrangements (in practice a notice). Everything else is a safety sign that the Health and Safety (Safety Signs and Signals) Regulations 1996 require **wherever a risk assessment finds a risk that cannot be avoided or adequately reduced by other means**, or a sign that a specific regulation requires once a condition is met: escape route and fire exit signs, fire-fighting equipment signs, signs at the entrance to stores of significant quantities of hazardous substances, Hearing Protection Zone signs, DSEAR zone signs, the gas emergency control notice, and fragile surface notices. Fire action notices, electric shock treatment posters, "Do not mix chemicals" notices, "Authorised persons only" and confined space warnings are customary or good practice rather than required in so many words, though an inspector would expect most of them in a pool plant room.

## Mandatory everywhere there are employees

### Health and safety law poster

**What.** HSE's approved poster, "Health and safety law: what you should know" in its 1999 edition (the edition on the wall in 2000 _(uncertain: edition title from memory)_), replaced in 2009 by "Health and safety law: what you need to know".

**Status: mandatory** (or the approved leaflet instead). Regulation 4(1) of the Health and Safety Information for Employees Regulations 1989 requires the employer, for each employee, either to keep the approved poster "displayed in a readable condition" at a place "reasonably accessible to the employee while he is at work", positioned so it can be "easily seen and read by that employee", or to give the employee the approved leaflet ([SI 1989/682 reg 4](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/1989/682/regulation/4)).

**Content.** Regulation 5(1) requires the employer to write on the poster, clearly and indelibly, the name of the enforcing authority and the address of its office for the area, and the address of the Employment Medical Advisory Service office; since 6 April 2009 the employer may instead give information on how employees can obtain those details ([SI 1989/682 reg 5, as amended](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/1989/682/regulation/5); [SI 2009/606 reg 2](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2009/606/regulation/2/made)). The 2009 poster has boxes for the names of health and safety representatives and other contacts; councils' guidance describes these as places where such details "can be added", and I found no regulation making them compulsory _(uncertain)_ ([Southampton City Council](https://www.southampton.gov.uk/media/iigk1elm/health-and-safety-law-poster-sep-2021-v1-02.doc); [University of Edinburgh](https://health-safety.ed.ac.uk/guidance/workplaces-general/health-and-safety-poster)). For the game's year 2000: the poster carries handwritten boxes for the local authority's environmental health office (the enforcing authority for a council leisure centre's leisure activities is the local authority, but for a council's own premises it is HSE _(inferred: local authorities do not enforce against themselves; check the Health and Safety (Enforcing Authority) Regulations 1998)_) and the EMAS office.

**Format.** A2 or A3; HSE sells both ([HSE Books A2](https://books.hse.gov.uk/product/9780717663149), [A3](https://books.hse.gov.uk/product/9780717663699)). Only the approved poster counts; HSE notes the current one has security features ([HSE law poster page](https://www.hse.gov.uk/pubns/books/lawposter.htm)).

**Where.** Wherever the staff will see it: in practice the staff room, the reception back office, or a staff noticeboard by the time clock. One per workplace is enough if every employee passes it; a site with separate staff areas often has more than one _(inferred from "reasonably accessible to the employee")_.

### Employers' liability insurance certificate

**Status: mandatory** for an employer who must insure. Regulation 5 of the Employers' Liability (Compulsory Insurance) Regulations 1998 requires the employer to display one or more copies of the certificate "at each place of business" where it "may be easily seen and read by any relevant employees", kept on display until the policy expires ([SI 1998/2573 reg 5, as made](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/1998/2573/regulation/5)). From 1 October 2008 the duty is also met if the certificate is made available electronically and each relevant employee has reasonable access to it, and the duties to keep old certificates for 40 years and to protect displayed copies from damage were removed ([Employers' Liability (Compulsory Insurance) (Amendment) Regulations 2008, via vLex](https://vlex.co.uk/vid/811784209); [CMS summary](https://cms.law/en/gbr/legal-updates/rules-to-change-regarding-employer-liability-insurance-certificates)). Note: local authorities are exempt from the 1969 Act's duty to insure _(uncertain: from memory of section 3 of the Employers' Liability (Compulsory Insurance) Act 1969; check before relying on it)_. A council-run centre of 2000 might therefore have no certificate; one run by a leisure trust or contractor (common by 2000) would.

**Where.** In 2000, a paper certificate (A4) on the staff noticeboard, usually next to the law poster.

### First-aid arrangements notice

**Status: mandatory duty to inform; a notice is the usual way.** Regulation 4 of the Health and Safety (First-Aid) Regulations 1981: "An employer shall inform his employees of the arrangements that have been made in connection with the provision of first-aid, including the location of equipment, facilities and personnel" ([SI 1981/917 reg 4](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/1981/917/regulation/4)). HSE's guidance L74 says "A simple method of keeping employees informed is to display first-aid notices", and "At least one notice in a prominent position at each site" should give employees enough opportunity to see them; it should be clear to all, including those with reading or language difficulties (L74, third edition, para 83: [HSE L74](https://www.hse.gov.uk/pubns/priced/l74.pdf)). A first-aid room should be signposted with a sign complying with the Safety Signs Regulations, and its door should carry a notice of the names, locations and telephone extensions of the first-aiders; first-aid containers are marked with "a white cross on a green background" (same, regulation 3 guidance).

**Content.** Names of the first-aiders (or the appointed person), where to find them or their extension, and where the first-aid box is. A green sign (white cross on green, ISO 7010 E003 today) on or over the box itself.

**Where.** At least one in a prominent position; in practice beside each first-aid box and on the staff noticeboard, and poolside at the lifeguards' first-aid point.

### Accident book (not a notice, but kept to hand)

Not a poster, but an employer with ten or more employees must keep an accident book (or electronic means) "readily accessible" ([Social Security (Claims and Payments) Regulations 1979 reg 25](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/1979/628/regulation/25)). In the game, a BI 510 book on the shelf with the first-aid box in the engineer's office or control room _(the form number is from memory)_.

## Safety signs in general

The Health and Safety (Safety Signs and Signals) Regulations 1996 require an employer to provide and maintain safety signs where, after other measures, it "cannot avoid or adequately reduce risks to employees except by the provision of appropriate safety signs", in accordance with Schedule 1 ([SI 1996/341 reg 4](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/1996/341/regulation/4)). They do not require signs where there is no significant risk ([HSE OC 212/1](https://www.hse.gov.uk/foi/internalops/ocs/200-299/oc212_1.htm)). The categories, from HSE's L64 ([HSE L64](https://www.hse.gov.uk/pubns/priced/l64.pdf)):

| Category                       | Shape and colour                                                                            |
| ------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Prohibition                    | Round; black pictogram on white, red edging and diagonal bar (red at least 35% of the area) |
| Warning                        | Triangular; black pictogram on yellow with black edging (yellow at least 50%)               |
| Mandatory                      | Round; white pictogram on blue (blue at least 50%)                                          |
| Emergency escape and first aid | Rectangular or square; white pictogram on green (green at least 50%)                        |
| Fire-fighting equipment        | Rectangular or square; white pictogram on red (red at least 50%)                            |

A sign must carry a pictogram; text may supplement it but a text-only safety sign does not comply (DCLG fire risk assessment guide for large places of assembly, Part 1 §"Signs and notices": [copy at Merseyside FRS](https://www.merseyfire.gov.uk/media/1748/guide_7-large_places_of_assembly.pdf)). Supplementary text should match the category: a mandatory sign says "must", e.g. "Face protection must be worn" (L64 para 46). Pictogram standards: in 2000, BS 5499-1:1990 and the 1996 Regulations' own pictograms; ISO 7010 was first published in 2003 and adopted as BS EN ISO 7010 in 2012 **(after 2000)** _(dates from memory)_. Escape route signing follows BS 5499-4 (L64 para 49).

**Containers and pipes.** Containers and the visible pipes carrying hazardous substances should generally be labelled with the hazard pictograms; labels go where staff are likely to be exposed, at sampling and filling points, valves and joints, without so many signs as to confuse (L64 paras 54 to 61). HSG179 says the same for pool plant: "where necessary, pipelines are marked either with warning signs or labels", decided by risk assessment (HSG179 para 193: [HSE HSG179](https://www.hse.gov.uk/pubns/priced/hsg179.pdf)). Colour banding of pipes by service to BS 1710 is **good practice**, not law; the game already does it (`pipe-markers.ts`). In 2000 the hazard symbols were CHIP's black-on-orange squares (Corrosive "C", Irritant "Xi", Oxidising "O", Dangerous for the environment "N") **not** CLP's red-bordered diamonds, which came in from 2009 and became compulsory for mixtures in 2015 **(after 2000)** _(dates from memory)_.

**Stores.** "Areas, rooms or enclosures used for the storage of significant quantities of hazardous substances or mixtures must be indicated by a suitable warning sign ... unless the labelling of the individual packages or containers is adequate for this purpose" (SI 1996/341 Schedule 1 Part III, as amended; L64 Schedule text). This makes a warning sign on the door of each chemical store **required if applicable**, and our bulk stores are the case it is written for.

## Fire

In 2000 the law was the Fire Precautions Act 1971 (fire certificates; a public leisure centre would have had one) and the Fire Precautions (Workplace) Regulations 1997; since 1 October 2006 it is the Regulatory Reform (Fire Safety) Order 2005 (England and Wales) **(after 2000)**. The sign duties are the same in substance.

**Escape route and fire exit signs: mandatory.** "Emergency routes and exits must be indicated by signs", and those needing illumination must have emergency lighting ([RRO 2005 art 14(2)(g),(h)](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2005/1541/article/14); the same in [Fire Precautions (Workplace) Regulations 1997 reg 5](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/1997/1840/regulation/5/made)). The fire exit sign goes "immediately above the exit opening" or where it is least likely to be hidden by smoke, and directional signs along the route wherever the exit cannot be seen (L64 paras 110 and 111). The enforcing authority may ask for "Fire escape keep clear" on doors that might be obstructed, such as a final exit into a yard (L64 para 113): the loading bay's exit is that case.

**Fire-fighting equipment signs: mandatory where not obvious.** Non-automatic fire-fighting equipment must be "easily accessible, simple to use and indicated by signs" ([RRO 2005 art 13(1)(b)](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2005/1541/article/13)); fire-fighting equipment is identified in red and by a location sign (SI 1996/341 Schedule 1 Part IV; L64). The DCLG guide says that where equipment is visible at all times, signs are not necessary. Extinguisher identification signs (white on red, with the extinguisher type) at each fire point are customary _(inferred)_.

**Fire alarm call points.** The call point itself is red and labelled (FIRE, "Break glass" or "Push here"). A separate "Fire alarm call point" sign is required only where the call point cannot be seen; customary otherwise _(inferred from art 13 and L64)_.

**Fire door signs: required by Building Regulations guidance for new work.** Approved Document B volume 2, Appendix C, para C11: fire doorsets are marked, to BS 5499-5, "Fire door keep shut" (kept closed when not in use), "Fire door keep locked shut" (kept locked), or "Automatic fire door keep clear" (held open by a release mechanism), on both sides, except doors to cupboards and service ducts, marked outside only ([ADB vol 2, 2019](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/677fa379d119b345376655eb/Approved_Document_B__fire_safety__volume_2_-_Buildings_other_than_dwellings__2019_edition.pdf)). The signs are mandatory blue. Strictly this is guidance for building work; the fire risk assessment keeps them in place afterwards _(inferred)_. Plant room doors that are kept locked would read "Fire door keep locked shut".

**Fire action notices: customary, not named in the law.** The law requires information to employees about fire procedures (RRO 2005 art 19), and the DCLG guide says notices must be used where necessary to give "the actions to be taken in the event of fire"; "Fire action notices can complement this information and, where used, should be posted in prominent locations", and in simple premises the emergency plan "may be no more than a fire action notice" (DCLG guide for large places of assembly, which names leisure centres and swimming pools in its scope: [Merseyside FRS copy](https://www.merseyfire.gov.uk/media/1748/guide_7-large_places_of_assembly.pdf)). By custom one is fixed beside each call point and on the main staff routes: white on blue (or red) headed FIRE ACTION, saying what to do on discovering a fire (raise the alarm at the nearest call point, call the fire brigade on 999), on hearing the alarm (leave by the nearest exit, do not use lifts, do not stop for belongings, do not re-enter) and the assembly point.

**Notices for the fire brigade.** The same guide lists notices "to help the fire and rescue service (e.g. location of sprinkler valves or electrical cut-off switches)" and signs indicating "the storage of hazardous substances". In practice: a "Sprinkler stop valve" sign on the valve room door, "Main gas isolation valve" and "Electrical isolator" signs, and a hazard sign at the chemical stores.

## Electrical

**Danger warning signs: required if applicable.** The Electricity at Work Regulations 1989 name no notices, but HSE's guidance HSR25 calls for warning notices among the precautions for live conductors and for "Suitable labels or notices" during isolation to prevent inadvertent re-energisation ([HSE HSR25](https://www.hse.gov.uk/pubns/priced/hsr25.pdf)), and the Safety Signs Regulations' warning sign for electrical danger (yellow triangle with a lightning flash) applies wherever the risk assessment finds an electrical risk. "Danger 400 volts" labels on switchgear come from BS 7671 regulation 514.10.1, which asks for a warning where a voltage over 230 V is present in equipment where it would not be expected; practitioners read it as voltage to earth, so a 230/400 V supply strictly needs no label, but a 400 V label is usually fitted anyway _(uncertain: forum discussion, not the standard's text: [IET EngX](https://engx.theiet.org/f/wiring-and-regulations/22974/panel-switchgear-yellow-electricity-warning-labels))_. Before 1995 the UK nominal supply was 240/415 V, so older switchgear in a 2000 plant would still say "Danger 415 volts" _(from memory)_.

**Switch room access: good practice.** "Authorised persons only" on the switch room door has no specific legal text today. The Electricity (Factories Act) Special Regulations limited access to areas set apart for switchboards to "an authorised person, or a person acting under his immediate supervision" (seen in the Northern Ireland version, reg 20: [SR&O (NI) 1945/113](https://legislation.gov.uk/nisro/1945/113/pdfs/nisro_19450113_en.pdf)); the 1989 Regulations replaced them. The sign survives as practice, and as a Safety Signs Regulations prohibition sign ("No access for unauthorised persons") where the risk assessment calls for it.

**Electric shock treatment poster: good practice now, formerly mandatory.** The Special Regulations required that "Instructions as to the treatment of persons suffering from electric shock shall be affixed in all premises where electrical energy is generated, transformed, or used above low-pressure" (NI 1945 version, reg 34, same source; the GB 1908 regulations had the same rule _(uncertain: GB regulation number not confirmed)_). They were revoked by the Electricity at Work Regulations 1989 ([HSE HSR25](https://www.hse.gov.uk/pubns/priced/hsr25.pdf), introduction). HSR25 para 224 now says: "You should consider placing notices or placards giving details of emergency resuscitation procedures in the event of electric shock at those locations where people may be at greater risk of electric shock", such as substations and test areas. In a 2000 switch room the old enamel or card shock poster is very likely still on the wall _(inferred)_.

**Controls and emergency stops: mandatory marking.** Controls must be "clearly visible and identifiable, including by appropriate marking where necessary" ([PUWER 1998 reg 17](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/1998/2306/regulation/17)); equipment must be marked with any marking appropriate for health and safety ([reg 23](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/1998/2306/regulation/23)) and carry warnings that are "unambiguous, easily perceived and easily understood" ([reg 24](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/1998/2306/regulation/24)). An "Emergency stop" label (red mushroom head on a yellow background) is the usual form _(the colour convention is from BS EN 418 / ISO 13850, from memory)_.

## Chemicals

**COSHH.** The Control of Substances Hazardous to Health Regulations 1999 (in force in 2000; replaced by COSHH 2002) require information, instruction and training, not signs as such; signs come in through the Safety Signs Regulations.

**Chemical store door signs: required if applicable** (Schedule 1 Part III, quoted above): a warning sign at the entrance to each store of significant quantities. For our two stores: the corrosive warning (in 2000, a yellow triangle with the corrosive pictogram, or the CHIP orange "C" symbol), with the store's name and contents.

**PPE mandatory signs: required if applicable.** Where the risk assessment requires eye, face, hand or foot protection, blue mandatory signs at the entrance (SI 1996/341 Schedule 1; HSG179 lists the PPE for chemical handling: respirator, rubber gloves, goggles or face shield, wellington boots, apron, para 253).

**Eyewash and emergency shower signs: required if applicable.** Green emergency signs (ISO 7010 E011, E012 today) over the eyewash and the safety shower, because they are first-aid facilities whose location must be indicated (SI 1996/341; L74).

**Spillage notice: HSE guidance for pools.** "The method for clearing spillage recommended by the supplier and the pool operator should be displayed on a notice together with the provision of the necessary equipment and its location" (HSG179 para 266).

**Fill point labels: HSE guidance for pools.** For bulk deliveries, pipework and connections are specific to each chemical, unique in size or shape, and "Loading points should be clearly labelled" (HSG179 paras 247 and 248). In the loading bay, each fill point is labelled with its chemical (SODIUM HYPOCHLORITE; HYDROCHLORIC ACID) in large letters, with the hazard symbol, and usually colour-coded _(colour coding inferred)_.

**"Do not mix chemicals": good practice.** HSG179 warns "Do not attempt to mix chemicals, even apparently similar types" (para 283) and explains how hypochlorite and acid release chlorine gas (paras 262 and 263), but I found no requirement to post it as a notice. A "Never mix acid and hypochlorite" sign at each store and at the dosing tanks is customary _(inferred)_.

**Plant room security: HSE guidance.** "The plant room should be a secure area for authorised staff only" (HSG179 para 251), which is the basis for "Authorised personnel only" on plant room doors.

**DSEAR zone signs: required if applicable, probably not here.** Where explosive atmospheres may occur, places are classified into zones and, where necessary, marked at their points of entry with the sign in Schedule 4: a yellow triangle, black-edged, with "EX" ([DSEAR 2002 reg 7](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2002/2776/regulation/7); [Schedule 4](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2002/2776/schedule/4)) **(after 2000: DSEAR came into force in 2002/2003)**. Sodium hypochlorite and hydrochloric acid are not flammable, so the chemical stores need no Ex sign. A naturally ventilated boiler house with welded gas pipework is usually zoned "negligible extent", needing no sign _(inferred)_.

## Gas and the boiler house

**Emergency control notice: required by the Gas Safety (Installation and Use) Regulations 1998.** Where the emergency control is not next to the meter, a "suitably worded notice in permanent form indicating the procedure to be followed in the event of an escape of gas" is prominently displayed on or near it; its handle is marked to show open and shut (or a notice says so), and any handwheel shows the direction of opening ([GSIUR 1998 reg 9](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/1998/2451/regulation/9)). Where a primary meter feeds secondary meters, a permanent line diagram of the meters, pipework and emergency controls is displayed at the primary meter ([reg 17](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/1998/2451/regulation/17)). In a boiler house of 2000, the customary set is _(inferred)_: "Gas emergency control valve" on the valve, the gas escape notice ("If you smell gas ... telephone Transco 0800 111 999"), the line diagram at the meter, "Boiler house: authorised persons only", "No smoking or naked flames" (prohibition), the boiler emergency stop (fire valve or "boiler house emergency shut-off") labelled at the door, and "Hot surfaces" warnings.

## Noise, PPE, confined spaces and the rest

**Hearing Protection Zone signs: required if applicable.** Where staff are likely to be exposed at or above the upper exposure action value, the area is a Hearing Protection Zone, "demarcated and identified by means of the sign specified for the purpose of indicating that ear protection must be worn" ([Control of Noise at Work Regulations 2005 reg 7](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2005/1643/regulation/7)) **(after 2000; in 2000 the Noise at Work Regulations 1989 had "ear protection zones" marked the same way _(from memory)_)**. Candidates in our building: the air handling plant and the boiler house when all burners fire; pump rooms are usually under the limit _(inferred)_.

**Confined spaces: good practice.** The Confined Spaces Regulations 1997 require safe systems of work and emergency arrangements, not signs ([SI 1997/1713](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/1997/1713/contents)); HSG179 para 193 says entry is "carefully controlled under a safe system of work". A "Danger: confined space, entry by permit only" sign on balance tank manways is customary _(inferred)_.

**Asbestos: if applicable.** In 2000 there was no duty to manage asbestos in premises; that came with the Control of Asbestos at Work Regulations 2002 reg 4, in force May 2004 **(after 2000)** ([SI 2002/2675 reg 4](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2002/2675/regulation/4)). Asbestos lagging labelled with the red-and-black "a" warning is plausible on older pipework in a 1970s building _(inferred)_.

**Fragile surfaces: required if applicable.** Where anyone may pass across or near a fragile surface, "prominent warning notices are so far as is reasonably practicable affixed at the approach" ([Work at Height Regulations 2005 reg 9(3)](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2005/735/regulation/9)) **(after 2000)**. For the air plant's gantries, if any roof lights or ceiling panels are walkable-looking.

**Manual handling, lone working: no sign duty.** The Manual Handling Operations Regulations 1992 and the general duties behind lone working require assessment and procedures, not notices. A lone-working procedure or check-in sheet on the control room wall is a reasonable prop _(inferred)_.

**No smoking.** In 2000, "No smoking" signs were a Safety Signs Regulations prohibition sign wherever smoking was a fire or chemical risk (boiler house, chemical stores). The statutory A5 "No smoking. It is against the law to smoke in these premises" sign at each entrance is **(after 2000)**: [Smoke-free (Signs) Regulations 2007 reg 2](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2007/923/regulation/2), from 1 July 2007 in England.

**Loading bay traffic.** Traffic routes are marked by continuous stripes, preferably white or yellow, and signs for vehicles where needed (SI 1996/341 Schedule 1 Part VI; L64). The loading bay wants a pedestrian/vehicle demarcation on its floor and a "Fire exit keep clear" on its exit door.

## Pools and spas

**Pool Safety Operating Procedures (PSOP): written documents, not posters.** HSG179 (fourth edition; the first edition, 1999, was current in 2000 _(edition date from memory)_) says a PSOP consists of the Normal Operating Plan (daily operation, layout, equipment, user groups) and the Emergency Action Plan (instructions to all staff in an emergency), which staff must understand and be checked on (HSG179 paras 28 to 33). Neither is required to be posted, but the EAP's key actions are commonly displayed at the lifeguard station and the plant room's chlorine gas emergency procedure on the plant room door _(inferred)_.

**Public notices: HSE guidance.** HSG179 para 58: safety signs warn the public of sudden changes in depth (depth marked at shallow and deep ends), areas unsafe for swimming or diving, slippery surfaces, and how to use the pool and its equipment; operators should keep signs unobstructed and the water at the depth displayed (para 59). Para 73: notices "at reception, in changing areas and on the poolside". Para 155: "Prohibition signs should be displayed when and/or where you have identified it is not safe to dive", with pictorial notices on safe diving (para 156). Unsupervised pools need signs at the entrance, in the changing rooms and in the pool area saying so. BS EN 15288-1 and -2 (2008) and ISO 20712 water safety signs **(after 2000)** set the modern form; in 2000, depth markings were painted or tiled figures on the pool edge and wall, and "No diving" a red prohibition sign _(standards' content not checked: both are paywalled)_.

**Spa (jacuzzi): HSE guidance.** HSG282 (2017; **after 2000**: the 2000 equivalent was the PHLS/HSE spa guidance _(uncertain)_) says notices to warn users help control the risk of heat, and that the design bather load and recommended bather duration should not be exceeded ([HSE HSG282](https://www.hse.gov.uk/pubns/priced/hsg282.pdf)). Customary spa notice: maximum number of bathers, maximum time (commonly 15 minutes), shower first, not for children or after alcohol, seek medical advice if pregnant or with heart conditions _(content customary, not prescribed)_.

**Water test results to the public: good practice only.** I found no UK law, and nothing in PWTAG's Code of Practice, requiring the results to be displayed ([PWTAG CoP 2021, Guernsey copy](https://www.gov.gg/CHttpHandler.ashx?id=191649&p=0) contains no such requirement on a text search _(uncertain: search, not a full read)_). Many pools show them on a poolside board; the game already has one (`pool-info-sign.ts`; `doc/research/pool-info-sign.md`).

## Placement in our building

The building has no staff room and no reception yet. Notices that would normally go there (the law poster, the insurance certificate, the main first-aid notice, the health and safety policy statement) need a home: I suggest a **staff noticeboard in the engineer's office**, the nearest thing to a staff base, with the law poster repeated in the **control room**, where staff on shift spend the most time. When a staff room and reception are built, the law poster, certificate and first-aid notice move (or are copied) there, and the public notices (pool rules, spa rules, water results summary, the public fire action notice) go at reception.
