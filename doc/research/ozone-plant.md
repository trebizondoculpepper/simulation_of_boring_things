# Ozone treatment plant: how it works and what to model

Research for an ozone plant in the main pool's plant room, as a large UK public leisure-centre pool might have had around 2000. Researched 2026-10-20. Every fact carries its source; anything marked **(inferred)** is my reasoning from the sourced figures, not something a source states. The key UK reference, PWTAG's book _Swimming Pool Water: Treatment and Quality Standards for Pools and Spas_, is not online; the figures below come from PWTAG's Code of Practice (2019), which summarises it, and from German DIN 19643 practice, which UK designers followed.

## 1. How pool ozone treatment works

**What it is for.** Ozone is a secondary disinfectant: it kills chlorine-resistant organisms (notably _Cryptosporidium_), destroys chloramines and lets the pool run on a lower chlorine residual. PWTAG asks for full-flow treatment, with separate contact and deozonising stages, at least two minutes' contact and 1 mg/l of ozone in the water circulated ([PWTAG Code of Practice 2019, §3.5 and §6.8](https://watermanenvironmental.co.uk/wp-content/uploads/2020/04/PWTAG-Code-of-Practice.pdf)). Above 32 °C the contact time rises to 3 minutes and the dose to 1.2–1.5 mg/l (same source, §6.8). 1 mg/l is 1 g per m³ of circulation flow, so the generator's output in g/h is the dose times the flow in m³/h.

**The three layouts PWTAG names** (same source, §6.8): ozone after filtration with separate contact and deozonising vessels, which it calls "the most efficient system, and best at disinfection"; after filtration with a combined contact and deozonising vessel; and before filtration, with filtration, contact and deozonisation combined in one vessel (the multilayer filter, where a layer of activated carbon on top of the sand destroys the residual ozone; [Hartwig 1996](https://www.poolhelp.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/03/JSPSI_V1N2_pp-26-33.pdf)).

**The German code UK practice followed.** DIN 19643's ozone process is coagulation, filtration, ozonation, granular activated carbon (GAC) filtration, then chlorination, applied to all the circulated water. Its design values: applied dose 0.8–1.0 mg/l at 28 °C, 1.0–1.2 mg/l above; ozone in the generator gas over 18 g/m³; contact time 2 minutes; at most 0.05 mg/l of ozone in the pool water. Off-gassing into the pool hall is not allowed ([Hartwig 1996](https://www.poolhelp.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/03/JSPSI_V1N2_pp-26-33.pdf)). Hartwig adds that plants are designed to those values but most inject only 30–50 % of design output in service, the generator being turned down by redox (ORP) control.

**Full flow and side stream.** Full-flow ozonation contacts all the circulated water; side-stream systems treat only part of it (custom designs about a third, packaged units often only 8–12 %). Either way, almost every pool plant injects the ozone through a side stream with its own booster pump ([Hartwig 1996](https://www.poolhelp.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/03/JSPSI_V1N2_pp-26-33.pdf)). PWTAG describes the same thing: a booster pump forces pool water through an injector, which draws the air and ozone from the generator into a mixer ([PWTAG CoP §6.8](https://watermanenvironmental.co.uk/wp-content/uploads/2020/04/PWTAG-Code-of-Practice.pdf)). The venturi's suction is what pulls the gas through the generator; if the water flow through it is too low, the vacuum and the mixing fail and little ozone dissolves ([Rice 1995](https://www.poolhelp.com/wp-content/uploads/2017/05/JSPSI_V1N1_pp25-44.pdf)).

**Where it sits in the circulation.** Because ozone (and UV) destroy chlorine, "dosing is always after the secondary treatment" ([PWTAG CoP, "Where to dose"](https://watermanenvironmental.co.uk/wp-content/uploads/2020/04/PWTAG-Code-of-Practice.pdf)). So the order is: balance tank, pumps, (coagulant dose), sand filters, ozone injection, contact tank, deozonising carbon filter, then chlorine and pH dosing on the way back to the pool. Where the heat exchanger goes is not stated in the sources; putting it after the deozoniser, so the contact tank and carbon see pool-temperature water and the heater never sees ozone, is **(inferred)** and is the usual arrangement in DIN-style schematics.

**Why the carbon filter.** Ozone is toxic and must not reach the pool; the deozoniser removes it from the water before it returns ([PWTAG CoP glossary, "De-ozonation"](https://watermanenvironmental.co.uk/wp-content/uploads/2020/04/PWTAG-Code-of-Practice.pdf)). GAC is the usual medium, about 0.5 m deep; heat-treated anthracite also works but needs about 0.7 m ([Bristol chemistry student project, 2001](https://www.chm.bris.ac.uk/~paulmay/webprojects2001/morgan/deozonation.htm), indicative only). The carbon also absorbs chlorine, so ozone does not save much chlorine on a properly deozonised plant ([Hartwig 1996](https://www.poolhelp.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/03/JSPSI_V1N2_pp-26-33.pdf)). The flow through a GAC bed is limited to about 37 m/h, which sets the vessel's diameter (same source). Post-filter contact vessels with GAC need only a few backwashes a year (same source).

**Why chlorine is still kept.** Ozone leaves no residual in the pool, so a chlorine residual is still needed to disinfect between passes. PWTAG says the free chlorine can be "substantially lower than with chlorine alone": about 0.5 mg/l in theory, 1 mg/l as a more realistic target ([PWTAG CoP §6.8](https://watermanenvironmental.co.uk/wp-content/uploads/2020/04/PWTAG-Code-of-Practice.pdf)). The first UK leisure-centre pools on ozone with chlorine (Northern Ireland, late 1970s) needed about 0.8 mg/l of free chlorine to stay bacteriologically satisfactory, with much lower combined chlorine than chlorine alone ([Wyatt and Wilson 1979](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/epidemiology-and-infection/article/bacteriological-investigation-of-two-leisure-centre-swimming-pools-disinfected-with-ozone/9A5AF17C574037DC0F666719ED06EA32)).

**Exposure limit.** The UK workplace exposure limit for ozone is a short-term (15-minute) limit of 0.2 ppm, 0.4 mg/m³, with no 8-hour limit listed ([HSE EH40/2005, fourth edition 2020, Table 1](https://www.hse.gov.uk/pubns/priced/eh40.pdf); [HSE EH38](https://www.hse.gov.uk/pubns/eh38.pdf), para 16). PWTAG's Code gives "0.2 mg/m³" as the threshold limiting value; that looks like a ppm figure written in the wrong unit **(inferred)**. PWTAG also says 10 mg/m³ causes serious irritation and breathing difficulty. Around 2000 the limit was an occupational exposure standard under an earlier EH40 rather than a WEL (that regime started in 2005); I did not find the 2000 figure itself, so 0.2 ppm for the period is **(inferred)**.

**Monitor setpoints.** No UK pool source gives alarm levels. A US drinking-water code requires an alarm at 0.1 ppm and automatic generator shutdown at 0.3 ppm ([Wisconsin NR 811](<https://docs.legis.wisconsin.gov/document/administrativecode/NR%20811.54(9)(b)>)); a two-stage alarm at 0.1 ppm (warning) and 0.2 ppm (shut down, at the UK limit) would be a plausible UK setting **(inferred)**. Electrochemical sensors were the common, cheaper kind, about ±5 % ([Spartan, ambient monitors](https://spartanwatertreatment.com/ozone-generator-ambient-monitor/)).

## 4. Safety and failure modes worth modelling

**What PWTAG says to watch.** The Code lists ten conditions, each to be wired to an alarm: cooling-water temperature; high-tension transformer temperature; air-feed quality; loss of vacuum; door protection switch; air flow; current to the regenerator, booster pump and HV transformer; PLC battery failure; the voltage/current relationship; and the interlock with the water flow being treated. The room needs a labelled emergency stop by the door, good ventilation, a lockable door and a restricted-access sign, and must not be a permanent place of work. Leak detectors are "strongly recommended" for positive-pressure systems ([PWTAG CoP §6.8](https://watermanenvironmental.co.uk/wp-content/uploads/2020/04/PWTAG-Code-of-Practice.pdf)). HSE adds that ozonators should shut down on abnormal conditions, "for example air drier failure, cooling water failure, excess system pressure", and must not be restarted after a leak until its source is found and fixed ([HSE EH38 para 44](https://www.hse.gov.uk/pubns/eh38.pdf)).

- **Ozone leak.** A positive-pressure system leaks ozone outwards; a vacuum system draws room air in instead, which is why vacuum plants are called safer ([ProMinent 2006](https://www.hellopro.fr//documentation/pdf_prod/4/8/5/3163_bb5ed6ff12076d84d007f534ab8f940d.pdf)). The smell is not a reliable guide: even slight leaks numb it, so the operator wrongly concludes the leak has stopped ([HSE EH38 para 44](https://www.hse.gov.uk/pubns/eh38.pdf)). PWTAG's general rule for gas leaks is that staff should not enter the affected area ([PWTAG TN62](https://www.pwtag.org/gas-monitoring-in-the-plantroom-tn62/)).
- **Cooling-water failure or overheating.** Output depends on cooling-water temperature; the generator trips on loss of flow or high temperature ([ProMinent OZVb](https://prominent.com/en/Products/Products/Disinfection-Systems-and-Oxidation-Systems/Ozone-Systems/p-ozonfilt-ozvb.html), [PWTAG CoP](https://watermanenvironmental.co.uk/wp-content/uploads/2020/04/PWTAG-Code-of-Practice.pdf)).
- **Dryer failure.** Wet air reduces output sharply above −60 °C dew point, and moisture turns the nitrogen oxides formed in the discharge into nitric acid, which corrodes metals and the dielectric ([Rice 1995](https://www.poolhelp.com/wp-content/uploads/2017/05/JSPSI_V1N1_pp25-44.pdf)); conductive acid can draw concentrated discharges that puncture the dielectric tube (patent summaries via search, not checked at source). On a heat-regenerated dryer, a failed regeneration heater ("current flow to regenerator") is the usual way the air goes wet **(inferred)**.
- **Deozoniser exhaustion.** The carbon is consumed by reaction and loses efficiency with use ([a 1999 study of activated-carbon air filters](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10529990/)), letting ozone through towards the pool, where DIN allows at most 0.05 mg/l ([Hartwig 1996](https://www.poolhelp.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/03/JSPSI_V1N2_pp-26-33.pdf)). No figure for the carbon's life in a pool was found.
- **No flow, no ozone.** The generator is interlocked with the treated-water flow (PWTAG); a low booster flow also loses the venturi's vacuum, which the generator sees as "loss of vacuum" and trips on **(inferred from PWTAG's list and Rice)**.
- **Off-gas destructor fault.** Condensation can blind a catalytic destructor, so ozone vents to the room ([Spartan](https://spartanwatertreatment.com/ozone-vent-gas-treatment)).

**What the operator would see (inferred from the products above).** On the generator: mains and run lamps, an output setting or percentage (3–100 % on OZVa), gauges for air flow, vacuum or pressure, and cooling-water flow, and fault lamps for each item on PWTAG's list, with a common alarm and RESET. On the monitor: a reading in ppm, warning and alarm lamps, a horn, and a relay that stops the generator. Ozone cannot be stored, so stopping the generator stops the hazard at source ([HSE EH38 para 8](https://www.hse.gov.uk/pubns/eh38.pdf)).

## 5. Suggested blocks and ports (suggestions only)

These follow the block-and-port model in `doc/plant-model.md`; names and units are proposals.

- **Air dryer** (heat-regenerated twin tower): in power, ambient air; out dry air (Nm³/h, dew point °C); state tower A/B, regenerating, heater current; faults heater failed, dew point high.
- **Ozone generator** (after the Bono Zon BONa 2D, 160 g/h): in dry air (flow, dew point), cooling water (l/h, °C), power (kW), enable signal from interlocks, output setpoint (% or ORP demand); out ozonised gas (g/h O₃, Nm³/h); state transformer temperature, vacuum, voltage/current; faults for each of PWTAG's ten conditions; output falling with dew point and cooling-water temperature.
- **Booster pump and injector**: in side-stream water (m³/h, bar) and gas; out water with entrained ozone; the vacuum it produces gates the generator.
- **Contact tank**: water in and out (m³/h), dissolved ozone in and out (mg/l), off-gas out (Nm³/h, g/h O₃); volume sets contact time; ozone decays and is consumed by pollution.
- **Deozoniser (GAC filter)**: water in and out, ozone residual in and out (mg/l), chlorine removed; state carbon remaining, differential pressure; backwash input.
- **Off-gas destructor**: gas in; vent out (g/h O₃ escaping); fault on heater or wet catalyst.
- **Ozone-in-air monitor**: in room concentration (ppm, from leaks and vent); out reading, warning and alarm signals, generator-stop relay.
- **Room**: integrates leak and vent rates against ventilation to give ppm for the monitor, and a 15-minute average against the 0.4 mg/m³ limit.

## Gaps

PWTAG's book itself (1999 and 2009 editions) was not available; its sizing tables, residual-ozone limit for the pool and carbon specifications are therefore taken from the Code and from DIN practice. No pool-specific UK alarm setpoints, carbon life or contact-tank sizing standard was found. Period (around 2000) datasheets were found only for ProMinent (2006); Ozonia's is 2011, and nothing was found for Wallace & Tiernan ozone or for Triogen's own models of the period.
