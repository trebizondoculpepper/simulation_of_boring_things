# Central Control: what goes on the operator's console

Research for mission 092's Central Control (`doc/prompts/092-control-room-2010s.md`), the operator's console at the middle of the grand control room. The owner, 2026-10-27: find out what kinds of controls sit on the central consoles of power station control rooms and the like, then pick something analogous for the pool; "water quality indicators, something that triggers the emergency alarm, whether the fire safety system is triggered, maybe the number of people occupying each pool hall, the time, that's a big one"; and in the final version everything on it is plumbed into the real system and drives the model. Written 2026-10-27 against the code as it stands. Claims drawn from the sources at the end are cited; anything marked **(general knowledge)** is from the author's background knowledge of control room practice and was not confirmed by a source found today.

## 1. What real central consoles carry

### Power stations (coal, gas: the unit desk)

The CEGB's big coal stations gave each generating unit a control desk with the panels behind or around it. At Cottam (4 × 500 MW, 1968) each unit had "a semi-circular control desk, above which is the alarm fascia suspended from the ceiling", with "a semi-circular instrument panel" behind it, and "all unit operation covering start-up, loading and shut-downs are carried out by the unit operator at the control desk" (Bedfordshire Archives, George Kent collection). Staythorpe B instead put three unit panels and a central loading desk in one plant control room between boilers and turbines (powerstationsrevisited). The Cottam unit desk itself is in the Science Museum's collection.

The split that recurs, as the archive captions describe it: the desk carries what the unit operator does all shift (raise and lower load, start and stop the unit, the main auxiliaries, alarm acceptance) with the alarm fascia above the eye line; the vertical panels carry the many indicators and the less frequent controls. **(General knowledge)** On a CEGB unit desk this meant: the unit load and the grid frequency (the National Grid control's load instructions arrived by phone or a dispatch instruction display), main steam pressure and temperature, generator MW and MVAr, turbine speed, the boiler's firing (mills in service), the feed pumps, the turbine and boiler trip pushbuttons, alarm accept and reset, a lamp test, a telephone panel with direct lines to the grid control centre and the plant areas, and the station's clock.

### Nuclear main control rooms

US practice puts the operators at a benchboard (a sloping desk in front of vertical boards), with the safety-related controls together and the reactor trip at hand. An NRC detailed control room design review found "two reactor trip switches on the bench panel, each turned in a different direction to trip the reactor" (two switches, deliberately different, so one slip cannot trip it and either operator can); another flagged controls on vertical boards about 30 inches back from the benchboard's front edge as hard for shorter operators to reach (NRC review reports). A Westinghouse compact control room patent gives each operator workstation dedicated pushbuttons for critical events, among them a reactor trip that goes through the safety system, so the operator never has to page through VDU screens to find it; a master console carries the reactor coolant, chemical and volume control, core, feedwater and turbine; a separate safety console sits to one side and an auxiliary systems console to the other, with a large overview display visible from anywhere in the room (US 5361198). NUREG-0700, the NRC's Human-System Interface Design Review Guideline (rev. 2, 2002), with NUREG-0711 the review model behind it, is the standard these reviews apply.

Sizewell B (a Westinghouse PWR, the UK's one) has its reactor operator seated inside a console in the middle of the room watching "many screens and dials" (Sizewell C supply chain). A visitor's account of its full-scope simulator, a 1:1 copy of the real room, describes beige L-shaped banks of illuminated switches; a back panel with gauges on its upright and red toggle switches on its desk, each under a cover against accidental knocks; the reactor trip a big round button at the bottom left; a joystick for rod control; switches that light when the plant disagrees with them, are turned to stage a change (flashing), pushed to commit it, and stop flashing when the plant agrees; alarms whose different tones let operators tell which are sounding; a purple border round the desks nobody crosses without the operations manager's leave, and a red line where visitors wait (bentasker.co.uk).

### Process plants and the standards

EEMUA 191 (alarm systems, 4th edition 2024; aligned with ISA-18.2 and IEC 62682) and EEMUA 201 (control rooms and HMIs, 3rd edition 2018) are the UK process industry's guides. EEMUA 201 covers console furniture, desk size, chairs, console arrangement and HMI navigation; its alarm side defers to 191 (EEMUA; PDSVision course outline). The display hierarchy that 201 and ISA-101 recommend tops out at a large-screen plant overview showing the priority-1 alarm groups and the key performance indicators, with no navigation and no control from it; in Halden trials a high-performance HMI let operators catch an abnormal situation before any alarm in 48 % of trials, against 10 % with a traditional one (Nimmo, IFE 2018). ISO 11064 is the control centre ergonomics series: part 3 the room layout and off-workstation (wall) displays, part 4 workstation layout and dimensions (reach and sight lines), part 5 displays and controls. Machinery-safety practice (IEC 60204-1, ISO 13850) wants an emergency stop at every operator control station where shutdown might be needed, red, mushroom-headed, self-latching, never bypassed (Banner and Turck datasheets).

### Water and wastewater works

A works or a regional water control centre is a SCADA desk: the field devices at the tanks and pumping stations, the HMI at the operator's desk, watching tank levels, pressures, flows, pump status and chlorine residual (open-exam-prep, water distribution). On a treatment works the values that matter are the final water's: free chlorine, turbidity, pH, and coagulant residual. The Scottish regulator's incident reports show why the console must make these unmissable and the telemetry's own health visible: at Roberton a low-pH alarm went to a standby operator who took it for false, a turbidity alarm followed, and three hours passed before anyone reached site, while residual aluminium exceeded its limit for about 16 hours; at Windyfield the works shut itself down because disinfection could not hold its chlorine, and no alarm reached anyone; at Acharacle a team leader was not told the site had no telemetry (DWQR incident assessments). **(General knowledge)** Water industry desks typically also carry a telemetry outstation status list (comms healthy or failed), the alarm list with acceptance, trend panes, and phones and radio to the mobile operators who go to site to put things right, which is very close to the game's rule that the control room diagnoses and the plant room fixes.

### Other analogues

- **NASA mission control:** a tiered room of consoles, each for one spacecraft system, all facing a large map screen (Wikipedia, Mission Control Center). The flight director's console was the room's centre of authority (Kraft designed the room around it, NASA). Its communications were voice loops: each controller could listen to any loop, usually about four at once, and talk on a primary one; the flight director's loop to the front room, front-to-back loops to each support team, a conference loop, and one loop to the spacecraft, spoken only by the CAPCOM (Patterson, Watts-Perotti and colleagues, 1999). **(General knowledge)** Each console also carried a mission elapsed time and Greenwich Mean Time display, event timers counting to the next burn or loss of signal, and a row of status lights.
- **Railway signalling:** the signaller's workstation carries the GSM-R radio terminal, with a Railway Emergency Call that reaches the signaller, the neighbouring signallers, route control and every train in the area; in the cab a red button sends "all trains stop", since fitted with covers against accidental presses. At Greenford a signaller did not send an emergency call because his terminal suggested it would not reach the train, and the RAIB recommended every train in or leaving the area always be reachable from the box (Network Rail; RAIB report 29/2014): the console must show whether the emergency command will work, not just offer it.
- **Fire alarm panels (BS 5839-1):** the user controls are SILENCE, RESET and EVACUATE behind access level 2 (a key or a four-digit code), and staff should silence and reset only "having first established the cause of the alarm" (Fike, Advanced, Olympia manuals). A repeater panel at a control desk shows the zones and may carry the same controls.
- **Leisure centre reception and the duty manager:** on a fire alarm, front-of-house stop admissions, secure the till and clear the phone lines; the duty manager goes to reception, reads the zone off the fire panel, treats an unexplained alarm as real, and evacuates or stands down by public announcement (Armagh Banbridge Craigavon, SLLC fire evacuation procedure). HSG179 (2018) wants a fixed or portable pool alarm wherever a single lifeguard supervises alone, everyone to know how to raise the alarm, and each alarm to sound distinct (summarised by Wirh). Pool alarm panels show which zone called and keep beeping every minute until the call point there is reset (Hoyles six-zone panel); wireless ones light an LED on a central monitor (RLSS Poolview) or page a staff pager (MDH swimSentry).

## 2. The principles behind the choice

Across all of these, what goes within the seated operator's reach is decided by a few rules:

1. **The first minute of an emergency.** The controls you need before you have time to think are physical, dedicated, at hand, and always work whatever the screens are doing: the reactor trip on the bench, the dedicated trip pushbuttons at every workstation (US 5361198), an emergency stop at every control station (IEC 60204-1), the GSM-R emergency call. They are protected against accident (covers, two switches turned different ways, a key, a confirm), and they show whether they will work (Greenford).
2. **What the operator watches all shift.** The few values that say whether the plant is doing its job (unit load and frequency; final water chlorine and turbidity) are on the desk's upstand or the overview, never a page away.
3. **Alarms are accepted at the desk.** The fascia or alarm list is above or before the operator, accept is under their hand; reset belongs where the cause is cleared.
4. **Frequent commands, not rare ones.** Start, stop, raise, lower and open, shut sit on the desk; set-up, tuning, mode changes, maintenance isolation and trip resets sit on the panels or in the plant (the game already has this rule: some controls are only local).
5. **Communications are part of the console.** Phones, radio and PA are in reach, because most incidents are handled by telling someone else what to do.
6. **One time for everything.** A master clock, synchronised to GPS or a broadcast time signal, distributes one time to the clocks, the SCADA and the event recorders, so a sequence of events can be reconstructed afterwards (WECC guideline: 1 ms at major stations; Schneider: NTP to 50 ms, GPS to 1 ms).
7. **Reach and sight.** The operator should reach the console without leaving the chair and see the wall over it (ISO 11064-4; the NRC reviews flagged controls set too far back and a console wing that blocked the view of a panel).
8. **Controlled access.** A boundary round the desks that others do not cross without permission (Sizewell B's purple border).

## 3. Alarm handling, communications, the clock, the logbook, CCTV

**Alarm handling.** ISA-18.2 and IEC 62682 define the alarm states: normal; unacknowledged (in, nobody has responded); acknowledged (seen, still in); returned to normal unacknowledged (cleared before anyone saw it); and shelved, suppressed and out of service, which need an explicit return (ICONICS, PyAutomation, OPC UA Part 9). EEMUA defines "accepted" as the operator having indicated awareness, usually by a push button. The hardware convention that goes with it **(general knowledge, the ISA-18.1 annunciator sequences)**: a new alarm flashes and sounds; SILENCE (or MUTE) stops the horn; ACCEPT turns the flash steady; when the condition clears it either goes out at once or, in a "ringback" sequence, flashes slowly until RESET; LAMP TEST proves the lamps. The game's annunciator already does ACCEPT, RESET and LAMP TEST (`src/toolkit/components/plant/views/control-panels.ts`). An alarm flood is "more alarms than can be physically addressed by a single console operator" (EEMUA glossary): the console's job is a short summary and a way to the detail.

**Communications.** A dedicated phone or intercom panel with direct lines (grid control, each plant area), radio to staff on the move, and PA to the public areas. Mission control's voice loops show the ideal of listening in without interrupting.

**The clock.** Every control room has a big clock in plain view, and the console has the time too: mission control kept GMT and mission elapsed time side by side. In a period setting it is a radio-controlled clock (in the UK the MSF 60 kHz signal from Anthorn) or a GPS master clock driving slave clocks **(general knowledge for MSF)**. The time stamps the alarm list and the log.

**The logbook and handover.** **(General knowledge)** Every shift keeps a log (a bound book, then an electronic log) of events, alarms, permits and actions, and hands over face to face against it; the alarm printer and the sequence-of-events recorder are the automatic halves of the same record. The leisure equivalent is the duty manager's log and the pool's plant log of water tests (PWTAG's two-hourly tests).

**CCTV.** **(General knowledge)** CCTV selection at a desk is a keypad or joystick choosing camera to monitor, and the incident's camera put up on a wall screen; pool CCTV is a supervision aid only, never a substitute for lifeguards (HSG179's position).

## 4. The console and the wall

The wall is the shared picture: the whole plant at once, the priority alarms, the key values, readable from anywhere, seen by everyone including the shift manager behind, and controlled from nowhere (EEMUA 201 / ISA-101 level 1; the MOCR's map screen; the US 5361198 overview). The console is where one person acts: the summary they need, the commands they give, and a selector deciding what detail the wall or their own screens show. The power station's version is the same with hardware: the vertical panels show, the desk commands. Central Control should be read the same way: the big wall (mission 092's LED process wall and starship viewscreen) shows the facility; Central Control commands it and chooses what the wall shows.

How the owner's ideas land:

- **Water quality indicators:** modelled (item 5): `world.pools[id]` has free and combined chlorine, pH, redox, temperature and suspended solids for all four pools, and the bands are in `health.ts`. Only turbidity as such is missing; `solids` is its source if a turbidity meter is added.
- **Something that triggers the emergency alarm:** not modelled (item 2). The annunciator has a `pool-alarm` window with nothing behind it. Needs a pool alarm in the world (call points per hall, a sounder per hall, raise from Central Control, silence, reset at the poolside).
- **Whether the fire safety system is triggered:** partly modelled (item 3). `world.fireAlarm` latches five call points in two zones; Central Control can show FIRE per zone today. The panel functions (silence, evacuate, reset), more zones and the annunciator's `fire-alarm` window are not.
- **Number of people in each pool hall:** modelled (item 6): `world.bathers.pools[id].now` against `CAPACITY`, for all four pools, through the day's timetable.
- **The time:** modelled (item 1): `world.time` from midnight Monday, with `clock()` and `worldClock()`; opening hours in `bathers.ts`. A real control room's clock is the single time the alarms and logs share, which the world already gives.

On the bridge version (dev46) the 092 split still works: left armrest items 2–4 and 7 (emergency and alarms), right armrest items 1, 5, 6 and 13 (time, water, occupancy, the viewscreen), the rest on the desk ahead.

## 7. Kept off Central Control

By `CLAUDE.md`'s rule and the real practice behind it (frequent commands at the desk, set-up and recovery in the plant):

- **Mode selectors** (HAND · OFF · AUTO): the motor starters' selectors (`motor/starter` selector), the chemical transfer's `distribution` `hoa` and `mode`, air units' `air/unit` `mode`, the spa filters' `spa-filter` MODE, the Olympic make-up selector (`olympic/makeup`), the wash controller's `mode` and `lockout`.
- **Trip and fault resets:** `motor/starter` `reset`, `distribution` `reset`, `air/unit` `reset`, `jacuzzi/cabinet` RESET, `plant/booster-reset`, the boiler's GAS RESET (`boiler-gas` `reset`), gas detection `gas/act` `reset`, lighting RCBOs (`lighting/board`), the fire call points' key reset, and the pool alarm's reset at its call point.
- **Isolation and supply switching:** the changeover switch (`plant/changeover`, local by its own design), feeders and isolators (`plant/feeder`, `plant/isolator`), the ECV (`boiler-gas` `ecv`), drain-down (`pool/drain-down`), the intake's valves (`intake`), deliveries, the battery's tests.
- **Releasing an emergency stop struck at Central Control:** best kept local too (a stop latched centrally released only in the plant room, as a real ESD reset is), which gives the player a reason to walk.

## 8. Gaps to close before it can be fully live

In the order the console needs them: a pool emergency alarm in the world (item 2); the fire alarm's panel functions, more zones and the annunciator's `fire-alarm` window driven (item 3); a dosing inhibit latch (item 4); alarm acceptance moved into the world so the annunciator and Central Control agree (item 7); the annunciator reading all four pools, not one (`world-reading.ts`); a remote stop distinct from the panels' mushrooms if the owner wants central stops released only locally (item 4); PA and intercom (item 15); the generator (`generatorAvailable` is always false). The features' stops in 092's draft (wave machine, flumes) have nothing behind them: the facility has no wave machine or flumes in the model, so they should be dropped or the features added.
