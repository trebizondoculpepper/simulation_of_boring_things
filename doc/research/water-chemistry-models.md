# Water chemistry models: what exists, what we built, and how it is tested

Researched and built 2026-10-28 for the owner: "We need to test the chemistry model in an abstract way before installing it into the game … we're going to have to add some kind of water chemistry model where the combination of things causes a tick … Perhaps this is one where we need some kind of background research to find out whether somebody has already provided us with a model that can simulate these aspects of water chemistry." The owner then decided the approach the same day: "I want you to take an existing study and then write our own version of the model and test it in the abstract", and that each circuit has at least two zones, the balance tank and the pool.

It follows `doc/research/pool-dosing-and-water-chemistry.md` (the chemistry in moles, the proposed state of its §7.1, cost and time acceleration in its §10) and `doc/research/pool-water-chemistry-and-biology.md` (the chloramine scheme, bathers, CO₂ loss, redox). Where a fact is sourced there it is not repeated here.

Conventions as in those notes: _(derived)_ is my arithmetic on sourced numbers, _(inferred)_ my reasoning, _(invented)_ a number with no source, _(fitted)_ a constant fitted to a reference model. Licences and repository activity were checked with the GitHub API and PyPI on 2026-10-28. Nothing here is installed in the world: `world.ts`, `pools.ts` and the rooms are unchanged.

## 1. Summary

**Nobody has published a pool water-chemistry simulator we can lift whole.** What exists is a set of pieces: PHREEQC, the US Geological Survey's public-domain geochemistry engine, which solves the carbonate system, calcium carbonate saturation and mixing exactly but knows nothing of chloramines; the Jafvert & Valentine (1992) chloramine scheme, coded under the MIT licence in the US EPA's Chlorine Breakpoint Curve Simulator; and pool-specific studies (Bristol's for PWTAG, Keuten, Schmalz, Wojtowicz, Berg) that add bathers, urea, trichloramine and carbon dioxide loss. The large frameworks (Reaktoro, WaterTAP, EPANET-MSX) are either licensed too restrictively, aimed elsewhere, or both (§2).

**So we took approach (b), as the owner chose:** our own small model in pure TypeScript, `src/game/water/pool-chemistry.ts`, built from those studies and checked offline against two references we can run here: PHREEQC 3.8.6 (through its MIT Python bindings) for the carbonate system, and a line-for-line Python port of the EPA simulator for the chloramines. Both reference runs are saved as JSON fixtures, so the tests need neither Python nor PHREEQC (§3, §4).

**The bench** (`src/game/water/chemistry-bench.ts`) steps abstract pools, each as two zones, pool and balance tank, with an ideal controller, pumps, bathers and fresh water, through ten problem-and-treatment scenarios in three phases each (steady, problem, treatment). All 40 checks pass, with their thresholds from PWTAG, TN23, CDC or the earlier notes; none of the model's constants was changed to make a scenario pass. Three test thresholds and two scenario procedures that I had first set wrongly were corrected, and each correction is recorded in §7. The owner's charts are an HTML report the bench writes to `build/reports/water-chemistry-bench.html` (§6, §7); it is not committed, being 2 MB of generated data, and is regenerated in a few seconds.

**The complexity answer: the owner is right about runtime and wrong about cost.** The full set (17 numbers a zone) costs about 1.1 µs a zone a step, so all four pools as two zones each come to under 10 µs; the minimal set would save perhaps a third of that. The real cost is in calibration and testing: the full set brings about ten more constants, six of them invented, and three of those visibly move the game (CO₂ loss, organic chloramines, thiosulphate's products) (§9).

**What the bench taught us** (§8): inorganic chloramine breaks point in minutes, not hours, so the combined chlorine a pool carries is organic and trichloramine; Wojtowicz's measured CO₂ loss drains a pool's alkalinity to 50–70 mg/l, below PWTAG's 80; PWTAG's thiosulphate dose implies a product that raises pH sharply; and dosing into the return keeps the pool about 0.15 mg/l above what the sample cell sees.

**Academic models of pool water** (none publishes code):

- **Davis & Bullock 2002, Bristol, for PWTAG** ([report](https://www.swimming.org/library/documents/3893/download)): Jafvert & Valentine as the backbone, plus a first-order chlorine loss and a urea term (k = 778.8 M⁻¹h⁻¹); verified in closed bottles; it over-predicts trichloramine at high pH, under-predicts dichloramine, and notes that DPD misreads chlorinated organics as combined chlorine. The model's urea constant is theirs.
- **Judd & Black 2000** (Water Research 34:1611) and **Judd & Bullock 2003** (Chemosphere 51:869): mass balances of a model pool; 2.8 g Cl₂ demand a bather; 50 ml urine and 200 ml sweat a bather; organic carbon, chloramines and THMs steady after 200 to 500 hours. Read through other papers' citations and summaries (earlier note §3.2); I could not open Judd & Black itself.
- **Keuten et al. (TU Delft, Water Research 2012, 2014)**: what a bather releases (37 mg urea, 10 mg ammonium, 77 mg total nitrogen in 30 minutes). Used for the bathers' nitrogen.
- **Schmalz et al. 2011** ([Water Research 45:2681](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21420139/)): urea's slow chlorination, a relative trichloramine yield of 76 % at pH 7.1, and its transfer to the air over 20 hours (rough water) to 5.8 days (still). Used for urea's trichloramine and the air term.
- **The Blatchley group (Purdue)**: urea as the main trichloramine precursor (Li & Blatchley 2007), gas-phase NCl₃ against bather load in four indoor pools (Weng et al. 2011, Indoor Air), real-time NCl₃ at a swim meet (Wu et al. 2021, ES&T), and a gas-phase model of hall NCl₃ with CO₂ as a ventilation proxy ([NACCHO webinar, 2023](https://www.naccho.org/blog/articles/webinar-indoor-air-quality-in-swimming-facilities-dynamics-of-gas-phase-trichloramine)). Its equations were not found published openly; the hall-air side is for a later mission (§10).
- **Wojtowicz 2001** (JSPSI 1(3):19, [via poolhelp.com](https://poolhelp.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/09/4-3-Factors-Affecting-Loss-of-CO2.pdf)): CO₂ loss second order in its excess, k in (ppm·day)⁻¹ by turnovers a day. Used as it stands.
- **Berg, Fang & Tang 2019** ([J. Water Health](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10086463)): 4.1 to 11 g Cl₂ a bather fitted to two real pools. Used for the bathers' demand.

**PHREEQC was run here.** `pip install phreeqc` in a scratch virtual environment gave PHREEQC 3.8.6-17100 with its databases; no Homebrew formula exists and R (for the EPA app itself) is not installed, so the EPA model was ported to Python with SciPy's LSODA, the integrator R's `deSolve::ode` uses by default. The two generators are `scripts/water-reference/phreeqc_carbonate.py` and `scripts/water-reference/jafvert_valentine.py`; their outputs are `src/game/water/fixtures/phreeqc-carbonate.json` and `src/game/water/fixtures/jafvert-valentine.json`. The port reproduces the classic breakpoint curve (§7.3).

**The project's own licence.** `package.json` declares GPL-3.0-or-later, so GPL code would in law be compatible; I have still treated GPL sources as reference-only, as briefed, and used none (question 1, §11).

**The studies the model is built on:** the Jafvert & Valentine scheme, as coded by the EPA, for chlorine and chloramines (reduced, and fitted to it); the textbook carbonate system with the EPA simulator's temperature polynomials and Davies activities for pH (checked against PHREEQC); Davis & Bullock for the pool's urea and background loss; Keuten and Berg for bathers; Schmalz for trichloramine; Wojtowicz for CO₂ loss; PWTAG's Code of Practice and TN23 for doses and limits.

## 4. The model we wrote

`src/game/water/pool-chemistry.ts`, pure TypeScript, no DOM, no Three.js, no world. Every constant is in one object, `CHEMISTRY`, each marked sourced, derived, fitted or invented, with its source.

**State, a zone** (`PoolChemistry`, the earlier note's §7.1 extended): free chlorine; inorganic chloramine; organic chloramines; trichloramine; ammonia; urea; fast and slow organic demand; total alkalinity; total carbonate; calcium hardness; TDS; sulphate; chlorate; cyanuric acid; thiosulphate; and pH, solved each step and kept only as the next solve's starting guess. Combined chlorine, as DPD 3 minus DPD 1 reads it, is the sum of the three chloramines (Davis & Bullock: DPD counts the organic ones).

**The step,** `stepChemistry(water, inputs, dt)`, in order: additions (grams of each chemical, by its moles and equivalents); bathers' releases; dilution (exact exponential) and evaporation's make-up (salts in, none out); the fast reactions taken at once (thiosulphate with free chlorine, ammonia to monochloramine); the slow reactions, each second order in HOCl and its reactant and integrated exactly over the step with HOCl held at its value then, scaled back together if they would use more chlorine than there is; first-order losses, exact (background, UV, trichloramine to the air, chloramines' own decay); CO₂'s second-order loss to the hall's air, exact; pH solved last. Alkalinity is kept by proton accounting with HOCl, CO₂, NH₄⁺, Cl⁻ and SO₄²⁻ as reference species, so a mole of hypochlorite ion adds an equivalent, every chlorine reduced to chloride removes one, breakpoint releases 1.5 a mole, and the measured alkalinity includes the hypochlorite ion as a titration does.

**pH** is solved from `TA = C_T(α₁ + 2α₂) + [OH⁻] − [H⁺] + [OCl⁻]` by safeguarded Newton, with Davies activity coefficients from an ionic strength of 2.5 × 10⁻⁵ × TDS. Derived readings (`readings`): HOCl, combined and total chlorine, dissolved CO₂, carbonate alkalinity, the Langelier index, TFP's CSI, calcite's saturation index, and redox by the earlier note's curve.

## 5. One volume or two zones (the owner's question)

The owner asked whether each circuit (pool, balance tank, pipework, filters) can be treated as one well-mixed black box, a dose dispersing quickly and acting by the resulting concentration and the temperature; and then decided on at least two zones, the balance tank and the pool: "When water with one chemical content is flowed into water of another chemical content, that is clearly going to change the content of the water."

**Mixing against reaction, with the game's own volumes and flows** (`plant-sizing`, `pools.ts`, `balance-tank.ts`, `spa-balance-tank.ts`, read and not changed) _(derived)_:

| Pool            | Pool + tank, m³ | Flow, m³/h | Turnover (whole circuit) | Tank's residence | One pass of the pool |
| --------------- | --------------- | ---------- | ------------------------ | ---------------- | -------------------- |
| Lounge          | 138.9 + 11.1    | 90         | 1.7 h                    | 7 min            | 1.5 h                |
| Olympic         | 2,500 + 216     | 620        | 4.4 h                    | 21 min           | 4.0 h                |
| Jacuzzi         | 2.83 + 1.1      | 8          | 29 min                   | 8 min            | 21 min               |
| Footbath        | 0.9 + 0.5       | 6          | 14 min                   | 5 min            | 9 min                |
| Bench 25 m pool | 470 + 30        | 167        | 3.0 h                    | 11 min           | 2.8 h                |

| Reaction                           | Its time scale at pool conditions | Against a turnover |
| ---------------------------------- | --------------------------------- | ------------------ |
| Ammonia to monochloramine          | 0.03 s                            | instant            |
| Thiosulphate with chlorine         | seconds to minutes                | instant            |
| Breakpoint of inorganic chloramine | about 4 min (15 × 0.9 mg/l HOCl)  | far faster         |
| Bathers' fast demand               | about 40 min                      | faster             |
| Bathers' slow demand               | about 4 h                         | comparable         |
| CO₂ loss                           | about a day at pool excess        | slower             |
| Organic chloramines, urea          | 18 h; 70 h half-life              | slower             |
| Trichloramine to air               | 20 h to 6 days                    | slower             |
| Dilution of TDS, chlorate          | weeks                             | far slower         |

**So one volume is right for everything slow** and wrong where a reaction is faster than the circuit: the fast reactions happen where the chemical goes in, not in the average. **What a single volume gets wrong:**

- **The controller's dead time.** The sample point is on the pump outlet, before dosing (earlier note §3.4), so the cell sees the dose only after it has gone round the pool: a delay of up to a pool pass, which in a single volume is zero. The bench shows it: a setpoint step from 1.5 to 2.0 mg/l overshoots to 2.17 mg/l with one volume and to 2.51 mg/l with two zones, and settles more slowly.
- **The local concentration at the injection point.** At full output the bench's hypochlorite pump (4 l/h of 140 g/l) puts 3.4 mg/l into the return main; the acid pump takes 3.4 mg/l of alkalinity there _(derived)_. That matters for keeping the two apart (chlorine gas when the main stops, which `gas.ts` already models as an event, not as chemistry) and for trichloramine, which forms where chlorine meets nitrogen in excess. Neither one volume nor two sees it; it is better left as an event in the world.
- **A hand dose into the balance tank.** With one volume it is in the pool at once. With two zones the bench shows the pool's alkalinity reaching 63 % of its rise in about 10 minutes, the tank's residence time, and all of it within the hour.
- **Stratification and dead spots.** A big pool is not well mixed: returned water reaches the far end over a fraction of a pass. Two well-mixed zones still put a dose into the whole pool at once; the owner's "about a turnover" for a dose to reach the pool is what a plug-flow or many-zone pool would show. The cheap remedy, if it matters, is a first-order lag on the pool zone's inflow (one more number a pool), or a third zone (the deep end) for the Olympic only.
- **The pool runs above its sample.** With dosing into the return the pool zone holds 0.15 mg/l more free chlorine than the tank's water the controller sees (the jacuzzi 0.56 mg/l); the DPD test at the pool and the controller disagree by that much without any drift. This is real (earlier note §3.1: the daily check compares the controller with the sample cell's water, and the two-hourly test is of the pool).

**What it costs.** Two zones double the chemistry (two steps of about 1.1 µs) and add an exchange and two pH solves (§9); the bench's whole two-zone step, controller included, is 8.7 µs, most of it the mixing's pH solves, which can be trimmed. A lag needs only the sample cell's own (60 s, already in `sampling.ts`).

**Recommendation and decision.** Two zones a circuit, as the owner decided: each a well-mixed volume with its own full chemistry; the circulation carrying pool water to the tank and tank water back through the plant (UV acting on it there) at the world's own flows; make-up into the tank; the controller's dosing into the return, after the plant; hand doses into the balance tank; the sample taken from the tank's water (the pump's suction) through the cell's lag, and no other lag. **The bench shows that two zones change no conclusion**: all ten scenarios pass the same checks either way (§7.2), and the differences are where expected: the loop's overshoot, a dose's arrival, and the steady 0.15 to 0.3 mg/l between pool and sample.

## 6. The bench

**Files:**

- `src/game/water/pool-chemistry.ts`: the model.
- `src/game/water/chemistry-bench.ts`: the harness (pure): `BenchPool` (volumes, flows, bathers, mains, air), `Plant` (pump sizes and strengths, setpoints, PI for chlorine and P for pH, sample lag, faults), `stepBench` (controller, pumps, hand doses, bathers, fresh water, two zones or one), `runBench` (events at hours, samples every 15 minutes), `warmedUp` (120 days from first fill, kept), `SCENARIOS` (each with its events and checks), `compareCircuits`, `handDoseThroughTank`, `setpointStep`, and `GAME_POOLS` (the four pools from the world's data).
- `src/game/water/pool-chemistry.test.ts`: stoichiometry, the PHREEQC fixtures, the J&V fixtures, invariants (40 tests).
- `src/game/water/chemistry-bench.slow.test.ts`: every scenario's checks, one zone against two, the hand dose through the tank, the loop's step, the four game pools steady (17 tests, about 13 s; a slow test, run locally by `npm test` and skipped on CI). `chemistry-bench.test.ts` keeps two quick checks from a fresh fill.
- `scripts/chemistry-bench-report.ts` and `scripts/chemistry-bench-report.test.ts`: the owner's report, run with `CHEM_REPORT=1 npx vp test run scripts/chemistry-bench-report.test.ts`, written to `build/reports/water-chemistry-bench.html` (ignored by git; `CHEM_REPORT_OUT` names another path): every scenario's charts (chlorine, pH, alkalinity and hardness, Langelier index, TDS, the pumps' outputs, and thiosulphate and sulphate where they matter), its three phases shaded and its events numbered, its checks tabled; the zones' comparisons; the game's pools; the references.
- `scripts/water-reference/*.py`: the offline fixture generators (PHREEQC; the J&V port).

**Invariants tested:** no species ever negative or not finite, at 600 s steps, under 2,000 steps of random doses (hypochlorite, acid, thiosulphate, soda ash) and bathers; chlorine conserved exactly (what is in the water plus what was reduced or went to the air equals what was there plus what was dosed); inorganic nitrogen conserved exactly (ammonia, urea, chloramine and trichloramine nitrogen, N₂ and NCl₃ to the air, against the bathers' releases); carbon conserved (carbonate, CO₂ to the air, urea's carbon); dilution exactly exponential; convergence as the step shrinks, and a busy day at 10 s and 60 s steps within 0.02 mg/l of free chlorine, 0.005 pH and 0.2 mg/l alkalinity of the same day at 1 s; the 10 s error no larger than the 60 s.

**How a model passes before it is installed in the world:** every bench and model test green (the slow bench locally, the rest in CI); the carbonate system within 0.03 pH and 2 % carbonate of PHREEQC; the chloramines within 0.1 mg/l (or 10 %) of free chlorine and 0.05 mg/l (or 30 %) of combined of J&V from an hour on; every scenario passing thresholds taken from a source, with constants unchanged to make it pass; step-size independence at 10 and 60 s; a cost within budget (a zone-step in microseconds); and the owner, having seen the report, accepting or asking to calibrate the invented constants (§11, question 2).

## 8. What the model showed us

1. **Inorganic chloramine does not linger.** In the J&V scheme ammonia meeting a pool's chlorine is through its breakpoint in minutes, leaving a few per cent as trichloramine. The earlier notes' "breakpoint over hours" is right only for the organic chloramines (bathers' amino acids, creatinine and the like) and urea. So the combined chlorine a pool carries, and that superchlorination clears, is organic and trichloramine, which is why the model holds them separately; their constants are the least sourced (question 2).
2. **CO₂ loss drains alkalinity.** With Wojtowicz's measured constant, scaled to each pool, holding pH at 7.3 to 7.35 costs acid enough that steady alkalinity settles at 53 (lounge), 67 (Olympic and the 25 m pool), 38 (jacuzzi) and 17 mg/l (footbath), all below PWTAG's 80, against mains water of 150. Real pools do restore alkalinity with bicarbonate (earlier note §2.4), but probably not this much; the footbath's figure is the formula extrapolated far beyond its residential pool (a surface-to-volume ratio ten times his). The constant needs calibrating against real UK plant acid use (question 3).
3. **Thiosulphate raises pH.** PWTAG's working dose (5 g per m³ per mg/l) is six times the sulphate stoichiometry, so it implies the tetrathionate product, which takes up acid; the hypochlorite's alkalinity then stays behind. After a 20 mg/l shock, pH went from 7.43 to 8.65. The earlier note's "releases acid as it reacts" holds only for the sulphate product. Either way TN23's "reopen when pH is back in range" is what the player must do (question 4).
4. **The pool runs above its sample**, by 0.15 mg/l (the jacuzzi 0.56) with dosing into the return (§5).
5. **Consumption is about right.** At steady state the Olympic takes 68 l/day of 140 g/l hypochlorite and 35 l/day of 10 % acid, within the earlier note's 50 to 75 l/day; its pump needs to be about 12.5 l/h (earlier note's question 9). The lounge takes 13 l/day and the 25 m pool 22.5 l/day (5.2 g Cl₂ a bather).
6. **Chlorate builds to 3.4 to 5 mg/l** at steady state from a product carrying 2 % chlorate (invented), removed only by dilution; the CoP sets no limit.
7. **Steady combined chlorine** is 0.10 (Olympic) to 0.33 mg/l (jacuzzi), and trichloramine 0.04 to 0.12 mg/l: plausible against published pool values, but resting on the invented organic constants.

**The answer: probably not, for the game's running; yes, for the work of getting it right.** At 1.1 µs a zone-step, eight zones (four pools, two each) add under 10 µs a world step against today's 33 µs for everything, and the slow species could be stepped every minute regardless of the world's step. What the full set really costs is calibration and testing: each extra species brings a constant or two and a test or two, and three of them (CO₂ loss, the organic chloramines, thiosulphate's products) decide whether the water behaves as a pool engineer expects. It also brings gameplay surface: hand-dosing everywhere, the weekly tests, laboratory results. The runtime cost is not where the complexity lies.

## 11. Questions for the owner

To be asked one at a time, in this order:

1. **May the model borrow from GPL code?** The project is GPL-3.0-or-later, so GPL sources (PyCO2SYS, for example) would be compatible in law; I have kept them reference-only as briefed. Keep that rule, or allow GPL-compatible reuse?
2. **Should the invented organic-chloramine constants be calibrated before installation?** They set the steady combined chlorine (0.1 to 0.33 mg/l) and how fast superchlorination clears it (under 5 hours); the alternative is to accept them as the game's choice and say so.
3. **What should we calibrate CO₂ loss against?** Wojtowicz's measured constant drains alkalinity to 50–70 mg/l in our pools (17 in the footbath). Real UK acid consumption figures for a pool, if the owner has any, would fix it; otherwise we cap the turnover term at his tested range.
4. **Which thiosulphate chemistry should the game follow?** PWTAG's dose (so mostly tetrathionate, and pH rises sharply after dechlorination), or the sulphate stoichiometry (six times less thiosulphate, acid released), with PWTAG's dose then a large overdose that leaves a demand.
5. **Soft or hard mains water?** It decides whether calcium chloride is a weekly job (soft) or never used (hard), and the steady alkalinity.
6. **Should the pool zone get a mixing lag**, so a hand dose takes about a turnover to show at the pool's far end as the owner expected, or are two well-mixed zones enough (a dose shows within about ten minutes)?
7. **Should the Olympic have a third zone** (the deep end, or the far end from the inlets) for stratification, or is that beyond what the game needs?
