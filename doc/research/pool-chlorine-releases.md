# Pool chlorine releases: acid and hypochlorite mixed

Research for mission 107i ("Never again: putting safety first", `doc/prompts/107i-never-again.md`), 2026-10-28. Real incidents in which acid met sodium hypochlorite at a pool (or, for comparison, at a delivery station elsewhere), and the guidance that followed. Each entry gives its source and how well it is verified. **Verified** means read in a primary source (HSE's own press release, an official incident database) or in several independent reports during this research; **indicative** means taken from a single secondary source, or carried from the brief's earlier summary and not re-read this session. Nothing on a museum panel may rest on an indicative entry until it is checked.

## The commonest cause

**Verified.** HSE's inspector, on the 2023 Basingstoke prosecution: "the accidental addition of acid to hypochlorite is the commonest cause of chlorine release incidents in pool buildings" (HSE press release, below). This is the one sentence the exhibit can quote as a real authority.

## UK pool incidents

### Basingstoke, Hampshire, 11 March 2021 (prosecuted 20 June 2023): verified

At The Hampshire Clinic's hydrotherapy pool, an outside engineer asked to lower the pool's pH added sodium hydrogen sulphate (sodium bisulphate, a dry acid) to the pool's sodium hypochlorite tank. Chlorine gas was released; he and two other workers were exposed. There were no trained employees for the pool plant and he was not fully trained in chlorine dosing or chemical storage. HSE's Science Division estimated the maximum chlorine produced was nearly 8,000 times the workplace exposure limit and nearly 400 times the IDLH limit. The operator, Circle Health Group Limited, pleaded guilty under sections 2(1) and 3(1) of the Health and Safety at Work etc. Act 1974 and was fined £160,000 with £12,520.03 costs at Basingstoke Magistrates' Court. HSE found no suitable risk assessments, training, instructions or safe systems of work for handling and mixing chemicals.

Sources: HSE press release, https://press.hse.gov.uk/?p=5101 ; PWTAG's notice, https://www.pwtag.org/?p=3808 ; SHP, https://www.shponline.co.uk/in-court/hampshire-clinic-fined-for-exposing-workers-to-chlorine-gas/amp/

Note for the exhibit: this was not a tanker delivery but a hand addition of acid into the hypochlorite tank; it shows the same chemistry and the same root failure (untrained hands at the chemicals).

### London Aquatics Centre, Stratford, 23 March 2022: verified as to the event, cause not established

A chlorine release at the Queen Elizabeth Olympic Park's aquatics centre. About 200 evacuated; 29 taken to hospital (Homerton, Newham, Royal London), with around 48 more treated at the scene (reports give 77 in all). London Fire Brigade: a "chemical reaction" released a "high quantity of chlorine gas". The centre's management said it happened when the facilities management company that runs the plant room took delivery of pool chemicals. No published investigation finding or prosecution was found; which chemicals met, and how, is not established in any source read.

Sources: PWTAG, https://www.pwtag.org/olympic-park-major-gas-leak-london-aquatic-centre/ ; PBS/AP, https://www.pbs.org/newshour/amp/world/chlorine-gas-leak-forces-london-olympic-pool-to-evacuate ; Leigh Day, https://www.leighday.co.uk/news/press-releases/2022-news/product-safety-lawyers-investigate-chlorine-gas-leak-at-london-olympic-park/

Note for the exhibit: the nearest real UK case to the fiction's "release during a delivery". Do not name it, or imply a cause, on any panel.

### Coatbridge, North Lanarkshire (Time Capsule): indicative

A chemical incident at the Time Capsule's pool left 33 people needing hospital treatment (Irish Times). An industry incident list (AIChE CCPS) gives Coatbridge, 2001, as a tanker delivery that put hypochlorite and hydrochloric acid into the same tank, with 30 treated. The year, the mechanism and the outcome were not confirmed from a primary source.

Sources: https://www.irishtimes.com/news/33-scots-injured-in-chemical-accident-1.322926 ; https://ccps.aiche.org/resources/tools/safe-work-practices/equipment-filling-and-mixing/incidents

### Wells, Somerset, 1989; Hinckley, Leicestershire, 1984; Manchester, 1990: indicative

Listed in an incident review annexed to a Hong Kong environmental impact assessment (EPD, EIA 134/2007, Annex D) as UK pool incidents in which hydrochloric acid and sodium hypochlorite came into contact; Wells as a tanker driver connecting the wrong pipe and discharging acid into the hypochlorite tank, eight treated. The annex's page returned 404 during this research and could not be re-read.

### Hednesford 2009, Sheffield 2016, Edinburgh, St Andrews 2015: indicative

Carried from the brief's earlier summary (Express & Star, 18 October 2012; SHP; Edinburgh Evening News; The Courier, 5 July 2016) and not re-read this session: Hednesford, a health club, sodium bisulphate and hypochlorite mixed by a contractor's engineer, eight or nine to hospital, operator and contractor each fined £45,000; Sheffield, a leisure centre evacuated after technicians mixed chemicals, £7,000 fine; Edinburgh, a health club fined £20,000 after chemicals mixed in a maintenance cupboard; St Andrews, a failed bulk hypochlorite tank (not a mix), 19 treated. ## Comparable incidents elsewhere (delivery into the wrong tank)

### Frankfurt am Main, Germany, 5 October 2007: verified

At a chemical wholesaler, while its filling and delivery stations were being renovated, a worker connected the transfer hose to the wrong tank: hydrochloric acid went into a sodium hypochlorite tank. About 200 kg of chlorine escaped before he stopped it; one employee was severely poisoned and died a month later, 54 people were treated, residents within 200 m kept indoors for two hours. The fixes: a separate hypochlorite filling pipe whose adapter has left-hand threads, so no other hose fits; every storage adapter locked, the keys released only after the laboratory has checked the load; every connection clearly labelled; a pH electrode watching the hypochlorite line.

Source: French ARIA database, accident 35830, https://www.aria.developpement-durable.gouv.fr/accident/35830_en/?lang=en

This is the best documented real example of the exhibit's lesson, "make the mistake impossible".

### Others: indicative

A US Chemical Safety Board case study describes an operator who unlocked the correct acid fill line but missed that a similar, poorly labelled hypochlorite line 45 cm away was also unlocked (The Chemical Engineer, https://www.thechemicalengineer.com/news/new-safety-video-from-csb-highlights-tank-fill-dangers/). Irish Water safety alerts record an unlabelled fill pipe assumed to feed hypochlorite that fed an acid tank (https://water.ie/sites/default/files/2024-02/IW-HSQE-SA-16_Inadvertent-Mixing-of-Incompatible-Chemicals.pdf).

## Guidance

- **HSG179, "Health and safety in swimming pools"** (HSE, fourth edition 2018, reissued with minor amendments, last updated February 2024): verified that this edition "no longer contains detailed information on swimming pool design or pool water treatment" and points to PWTAG. Its detailed chemical-handling advice is therefore not something the exhibit can cite from the current edition. Earlier editions (1999, 2003) did carry plant-room and chemical guidance; their wording was not read. https://www.hse.gov.uk/pubns/books/hsg179.htm
- **PWTAG**, "Swimming Pool Water: Treatment and Quality Standards for Pools and Spas" (the "Code of Practice" and book): the UK's working guidance on chemical storage and deliveries. Its text was not reached in this research; that it advises separate stores, distinct and labelled fill points and supervised deliveries is **indicative** (common industry practice, consistent with the incidents' lessons) until quoted from it.
- **COSHH Regulations 2002** and the **Health and Safety at Work etc. Act 1974**: the law under which Basingstoke was prosecuted (verified for the Act).
- **Couplings** (indicative, from trade catalogues and the FECC/Belgian distributors' coupling guideline, https://www.fecc.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/01/121119-Guidelines-for-Couplings.pdf): the camlock (cam-and-groove, EN 14420-7) has male and female halves and is widespread for tanker work; the Guillemin (NF E 29-572) is symmetrical, genderless, joined by a quarter turn, common in Europe for chemicals. Different families, and different sizes within one family, cannot be coupled, which is the principle of the fix. The FECC guideline groups couplings by chemical family (acids, alkalis, hypochlorite and others) so that drivers meet a consistent scheme.

## The failure modes the incidents share

Untrained people at the chemicals (Basingstoke, verified); a hose connected to the wrong tank at a station with look-alike connections (Frankfurt, verified; CSB, indicative); fill points poorly labelled or unlocked (Frankfurt, CSB, Irish Water); a release at delivery time (London, verified as to timing only). No source read here describes a disconnected gas detector at a UK pool; that detail in the fiction is invented and should stay unattributed.
