# 092: The control room, a bit of every era

Status: stage 0 under way (2026-10-27): the walkable prototype in two versions on one geometry, dev45 the Bond villain's lair (`?room=dev45`) and dev46 the starship bridge (`?room=dev46`, with its stations for each pool system and its ramps), to the owner's chosen layout as it grew the same day (below, "The chosen layout"); refinements next. Written 2026-10-27 from the owner's request; its layout and the screens question settled by the owner the same day.

You are working on "The Pool", a browser game in TypeScript and Three.js simulating a leisure centre's pool plant (this repository). Read `CLAUDE.md` first, and `doc/design/control-interface-archetypes.md`. Markdown is one paragraph per line.

## The request

The owner, 2026-10-27: "for the main control room, I want to have something that is dramatically different. I'm thinking like a late 2010s interface in which computer-based screens are starting to become normal. So there might be quite large LED matrix screens showing the flow of fluids and processes, animated stuff. It'd be really fun if we could bring back some satirical. I feel like bringing back Clippy as a mascot, like saying, 'It looks like you're trying to clear a filter bed.' So, maybe for the control room, I want to create something that has an aspect of a 2010s, mid-2010s, elaborate, modern-ish control room with period features of early computer-based displays."

## What there is now

The control room (`control_room`, a shell room, `layoutRoom` in `src/toolkit/gallery/logic/room-equipment.ts`) holds the operator's desk (POOLMON), an alarm printer, a water-test kit, wall mimics for the main pool circuit, the heating and the air handling, and an alarm annunciator: the 1980s large-panel archetype throughout, reading the one world state.

## The idea

A third archetype for the control room: the mid-2010s control room, where screens have arrived but are still a novelty, alongside what came before.

- **A video wall of large LED-matrix panels:** coarse, bright, pixel-visible displays (after the LED message and process-display boards of the period) showing animated process views drawn from the world: water moving through the circulation, filters, heat exchangers and chemical lines, flows and levels changing as they do in the plant. Its own simulated interface, drawn by the game (not a web page in a texture), each panel its own controller and its own failures (a dead tile, a stuck row).
- **Operator workstations of the period:** a pair of wide 16:9 LCD monitors on arms showing a SCADA-style HMI of the time (flat colours, tabbed screens, trend plots, an alarm banner), worked through the game's own control scheme, keyboard and mouse as props.
- **The assistant mascot, satirical:** an over-eager on-screen assistant in the HMI that pops up with unhelpful help ("It looks like you're trying to clear a filter bed. Would you like help?"), pointing at the wrong thing, dismissable, coming back. An original character in the spirit of the late-1990s office assistant, not Microsoft's Clippy itself (its name, likeness and artwork are theirs): for example a talking float switch or a cheerful test-kit pot with eyes, named in the game's own way.
- **The period features:** a wall clock synced by radio, a CCTV quad monitor, the alarm printer kept on as a backup nobody reads, laminated quick-reference cards, a coffee-ringed keyboard, a wall-mounted flat TV on a news channel.
- **What stays:** the control room still diagnoses; putting things right often means going to the room concerned (`CLAUDE.md`: some controls are only local). The screens show and command what the mimics do now (valves' OPEN and SHUT, pumps' START and STOP); mode selectors and trip resets stay local.

## The owner's answer: a bit of everything (2026-10-27)

"I want to kind of keep the bit of everything thing going. So the control room, I would like it to have a central desk with a sort of a console that puts some important controls within arm's reach of a controller sat at a desk. And beyond the controller's desk, which will have physical controls, there will be various kinds of wall controls or consoles, and these will be in different styles from different eras. So perhaps the discrete industrial components of the 80s, there will be the LCD displays of the 90s, and maybe a couple of computer-generated displays of the mid-2010s."

So the screens do not take over (the `CLAUDE.md` rule stands): they are a couple of the room's consoles among many.

- **The controller's desk, in the middle:** a seated operator's console with physical controls within arm's reach: the important commands (the pumps' START and STOP, the valves' OPEN and SHUT that the mimics carry, an alarm ACCEPT and RESET, the plant-wide stops), lit push buttons and selectors on its sloping desk top, a few key readouts on its upstand. Local-only controls (mode selectors, trip resets) still stay in the plant rooms.
- **Beyond it, round the walls, consoles of different eras, each after its archetype:** the 1980s discrete industrial components (the existing mimics and annunciator, large panels of 30 mm devices, moving-coil meters); the 1990s LCD displays (character and segment LCD panels, the early-2000s compact terminals of mission 091 and their like); and a couple of mid-2010s computer-generated displays (the LED-matrix process wall and a period HMI workstation with its assistant mascot, as above).
- Each console its own kind of interface, all reading and working the one world, so the room shows the same plant several ways.

## The room itself (the owner, 2026-10-27)

"I think we need to begin by prototyping the new control room. So, I have an idea that actually, to make it more dramatic, you would enter from the corridor, immediately go up a flight of stairs, which would take us an entire 8-meter story, and then it opens out into a seven-sided room. It'll be like a flattened heptagon with one large wall and a sort of symmetrical layout. And in the center of the room is a similarly shaped desk where the operator's chair is. Around the operator on all of the walls will be controls, projections, mimics, displays, the master control room for the entire facility."

- **The approach:** from the corridor door straight onto a stair that climbs a whole 8 m storey. UK rules (Approved Document K) allow at most 16 risers to a flight, so 8 m is about 46 risers of about 175 mm in three or more flights with landings: a switchback stair in its own stair hall (default; the owner may prefer another form).
- **The room:** a flattened heptagon, symmetrical about one axis: one large wall (the front, the main display wall), its two neighbours angled in, and four shorter walls closing round the back. With seven sides the axis runs from the large wall's middle to the opposite corner, the big wall facing the walker as they come up; the stair arrives off centre (the chosen layout, below).
- **The desk:** in the middle, a heptagonal operator's desk of the same shape (smaller, its large side toward the big wall), the operator's chair within it, its console within arm's reach.
- **The desk's reference (the owner, 2026-10-27):** the original EPCOT Center control room at Walt Disney World (EPCOT Computer Central, CommuniCore, 1982, sponsored by Sperry Univac), its extravagant desks with controls and displays set into them: long, sleek, low consoles facing a glass wall, operators seated, screens and control panels recessed into the desk surfaces rather than standing on them, rows of equipment cabinets behind, guests watching from balconies, projections over the glass. Described in [Progress City USA](https://progresscityusa.com/energy/tech_central.htm) and [Disney Avenue](https://www.disneyavenue.com/2015/07/epcots-smrt-1-and-computer-central.html); period photographs are in fan archives and RetroWDW's 1983 video. The owner's photograph (2026-10-27, kept out of the repository) shows the desk built of straight modular segments joined at angles into a polygonal run, which suits the heptagon: each segment a pale grey or cream sloping top with keyboards and panels set in, a raised angled upstand with CRTs recessed into it, a dark charcoal front and kick panel, dark side cheeks; red swivel chairs; rows of cream and terracotta mainframe cabinets behind; warm recessed downlights in a low dark ceiling; a warm brown floor; seen through tall glass with dark mullions. The prototype's desk follows it: one segment to each side of the heptagon, an opening at the back for the operator.
- **The walls:** every wall carries controls, projections, mimics and displays of the eras above: the master control room for the whole facility.
- **The chosen layout (the owner, 2026-10-27, from four concepts: "I like option C, but with some modifications"):** one big open space, no glass, no office. The operator's segmented heptagonal desk in the middle, facing the big wall. The back end, the triangle of the two back walls, an open platform about 0.6 m up, steps in the middle and a rail along its edge, with a conference table and chairs looking over the desk to the wall. The stair off centre ("more practical"), rising through a guarded opening in the main floor on the left. Mimics and displays on every wall, the back walls included.
- **Grandiose (the owner, 2026-10-27):** "lean into a sort of Bond villain meets Mission Control", with "a giant circular ring light over the main operator's chair, reminiscent of the war room from Doctor Strangelove"; then "The room might still be a bit small. I like the idea that the rear section is raised. It's up some stairs, and it needs to be big enough for a conference table with chairs on each side ... Make sure that the stairs have guardrails on them. We're sort of pushing now for an extreme level of grandiosity, which is inconsistent with the rest of the game." So: the big wall about 24 m, the room about 28 × 22 m, the ceiling about 9 m; a Ken Adam finish (polished dark floor, faceted dark walls, a deep coffered ceiling, the displays and desk glowing in the gloom); a ring luminaire about 8 m across hung over the operator's chair; the rear stage about 1.0–1.2 m up a wide central flight, at least 7 m deep and 10 m wide where a 5 × 1.5 m conference table stands with chairs along both sides; handrails and guarding on every stair and along the stage's edge.
- **The inspirations (the owner, 2026-10-27):** "the bridge from the Starship Enterprise of the Next Generation, Bond villain's lair, the Epcot control room, make it big and grandiose and inappropriate." Drawn on in the game's own designs, never their logos, names or artwork: the bridge's curved, wood-toned rail round the raised rear, its big front viewscreen, the captain's-chair focus and flat colour-blocked panels as a style cue only; the lair's dark polish and menace; EPCOT's segmented console desk.
- **A starship bridge for a swimming pool (the owner, 2026-10-27):** "it would be really funny if we could actually represent the swimming pool's operations in the design language of Star Trek: The Next Generation ... flat controls that are intended to be touch sensitive, with Star Trek style bleeping noises, a giant front facing full screen with operator stations for each of the major swimming pool systems." So: the big wall a full-height main screen; round the operator, a station for each major system (circulation and filtration, chemical dosing, heating, air handling, power, building: doors, CCTV and alarms), each a flat, sloping touch panel of colour-blocked rounded bars on black, every touch answered by a bleep. A parody in the game's own design, sounds and lettering: never LCARS's actual artwork, fonts, names or logos. Real and working when built: each station shows and works its system through the world, as the mimics do (local-only controls still stay in the plant rooms).
- **Two versions, two dev rooms (the owner, 2026-10-27):** one room's geometry, two finishes, from one shared builder: dev45 the Bond villain's lair (Ken Adam: a ring of lights hung low over the operator's desk as over Dr. Strangelove's War Room table, a pool of light and darkness round it, a glossy black floor, dark faceted walls, a deep angled coffered ceiling, brushed steel and black lacquer); and a second dev room the starship bridge (after the Next Generation bridge's design language: soft cove lighting, grey and taupe carpet, warm beige panels with rounded corners, a horseshoe wooden rail round the raised rear, the operator's chair in the centre with a station per pool system, a full-height main viewscreen, ramps to the rear). References: [Den of Geek on the War Room](https://www.denofgeek.com/movies/dr-strangelove-in-praise-of-one-of-cinemas-great-film-sets), [BFI, Ken Adam on his finest sets](https://www.bfi.org.uk/sight-and-sound/news/designing-dreams-nightmares-ken-adam-his-finest-sets), [Ex Astris Scientia on the Enterprise-D bridge](https://www.ex-astris-scientia.org/inconsistencies/enterprise-d-bridge.htm), [WhatCulture on the TNG bridge](https://whatculture.com/tv/star-trek-10-secrets-of-the-next-generation-main-bridge).
- **First:** prototype the room in a dev room (stair and all, blank consoles as placeholders where needed), before any consoles are built or the real control room changes.

## The chosen layout and its references (the owner, 2026-10-27)

The owner's choice, from the concept plans: **"E, option C as the owner wants it"**, then made bigger and grander the same day. One big open space: no glass, no office, no partition.

- **Size, deliberately grandiose:** "big and grandiose and inappropriate" for a leisure centre (the owner: an extreme level of grandiosity, inconsistent with the rest of the game, on purpose). A flattened heptagon 28 × 22 m: the big front wall 24 m, its two neighbours angled out to the widest, two side walls angled back in, two back walls meeting in the back corner on the axis; 9 m to the ceiling.
- **The desk:** in the middle front, facing the big wall, a segmented heptagonal operator's desk of the room's shape (one segment per side, the back two left open as the operator's way in), the operator's red chair inside it. After EPCOT Computer Central (Walt Disney World, CommuniCore, 1982): straight modular segments joined at angles, a cream sloping top with panels set into it, a raised angled upstand with its screens recessed into it, charcoal fronts and kick panels, dark cheeks; placeholders (dark recessed screen insets, blank panel insets) for now.
- **The ring light:** over the operator's chair a giant circular ring luminaire, about 8 m across, hung on rods, lighting the desk in a pool, after the war room in Dr. Strangelove. Set decoration.
- **The raised stage:** the back end, behind the line joining the two back side corners, a stage 1.05 m up, a wide central flight of six risers with handrails both sides, a 1.1 m guard along the rest of its front (a drop over 600 mm), holding a long conference table (5 × 1.5 m) with chairs along both sides, looking over the desk to the big wall.
- **The stair:** off centre (the owner: more practical): the 8 m switchback stair rises through a guarded opening in the main floor on the left, between the desk and the left wall, arriving facing the front. Guardrails on every stair: handrails both sides of each flight, a balustrade at the open landing edge, a glass guard round the opening.
- **The walls, every one:** the big wall the main display (2010s LED process wall); the two angled front walls 1980s mimics (pools; heating); the next two 1990s LCD consoles and OT-216 terminals; the back walls a 1980s mimic and annunciator (air) and 2010s trend and CCTV screens.
- **Stations for each pool system (the owner's next idea):** "in the design language of Star Trek: The Next Generation... flat controls that are intended to be touch sensitive, with Star Trek style bleeping noises, a giant front facing full screen with operator stations for each of the major swimming pool systems." Stations, one per major system: CIRCULATION & FILTRATION, CHEMICAL DOSING, HEATING, AIR HANDLING, POWER (ELECTRICAL), BUILDING (doors, CCTV, alarms), each a flat sloping touch-panel console; working touch controls and their sounds are a later stage. Flat colour-blocked rounded bars on black as a style cue only, in the game's own design (not Star Trek's LCARS artwork, fonts, names or logos).
- **Built (2026-10-27):** one shared builder with a theme (`views/control-room-prototype.ts`, `'lair' | 'bridge'`) on one pure geometry (`logic/control-room-prototype.ts`); dev45 the lair (no stations, a ring of photo-flood lamps 8 m across hung 3.4 m over the floor, triangular coffers, black lacquer and brushed steel, a dim wash), dev46 the bridge (six stations in a shallow arc round the front of the desk, each turned toward the middle but facing the big wall, a pair of chairs either side of the operator's, the full-height viewscreen showing a starfield placeholder, carpet, beige panels, cove lights, a wood-capped parapet with colour bars, and a ramp either side of the stage's steps).
- **The look:** three inspirations, as original designs (no copied logos, names or artwork): Ken Adam's Bond-villain sets (Dr. No, Goldfinger, You Only Live Twice: a polished dark floor, dark faceted walls, a deep coffered ceiling, the displays and the desk glowing in the gloom, menace and expense); the Enterprise bridge of The Next Generation (the big front viewscreen as the main wall, the operator's chair as the focus, wood-toned panelling and beige round the raised rear section, flat rounded colour bars); EPCOT Computer Central (the segmented console desk with screens set into it). Mission Control order: everything faces the big wall.

## Central Control: the operator's console (2026-10-27)

The console nearest the operator's chair is called **Central Control** (the owner). What goes on it: whatever matters in the first minute of an incident, or what the person in charge watches all day; everything else lives at the system stations. In order of importance, from what the game models:

1. **Emergency:** the pool emergency alarm and evacuate (with the fire alarm's status); STOP DOSING, all pools; stop circulation per pool (with a confirm); the features' stops (wave machine, flumes).
2. **Water quality at a glance:** free chlorine, pH and temperature per pool, each a green, amber or red tile (combined chlorine and turbidity when modelled).
3. **Alarms:** the annunciator's summary (active, unaccepted), ACCEPT, and a list whose items put the alarm on the main screen.
4. **Circulation and filters:** each pool's pumps running and flow; each filter in service, backwash due or backwashing.
5. **Chemicals in stock:** the stores and day tanks in litres; a transfer under way.
6. **Power:** mains or generator (the changeover switch), the emergency lighting, any room without supply.
7. **Building:** bathers per pool against its limit; a CCTV camera onto the main screen; doors and access.
8. **The main viewscreen's selector:** what the big screen shows (schematic, CCTV, trends, alarms).
9. **Comms:** PA to the poolside, intercom to each plant room.

Kept off it, by the rule that some controls are only local: mode selectors (HAND · OFF · AUTO) and trip resets. Central Control sees everything and commands what the mimics command; putting a trip right still means going to the plant room. On the bridge version, it may be split into the chair's two armrests (left: emergency and alarms; right: water quality and the viewscreen) with the rest on the desk ahead.
